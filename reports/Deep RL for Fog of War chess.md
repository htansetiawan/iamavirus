# Keep the Search, Regularise the Learner

The evidence points to a split decision: the arena bot should remain a search engine, and the training loop should stop being plain PPO. Every Reconnaissance Blind Chess (RBC) competition was won by a belief-tracking engine-evaluation bot, and the only superhuman Fog-of-War (FoW) chess system, Obscuro, uses no learned model at all, running sampled-infoset CFR with Stockfish leaves on three CPU threads at 3+2 clocks and losing only about **50 Elo when squeezed from 5 s to 1/16 s per move** ([Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2)). On the learning side, the Magnetic Mirror Descent paper shows that PPO self-play with default settings produces a policy **more exploitable than uniform random after 10M steps**, while a one-term change (reverse-KL regularisation with a magnet) drives exploitability down ([MMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)); and Baisero & Amato prove that a critic conditioned on the true board alone is a biased baseline under partial observability, which is the most likely explanation of a value head stuck near chance ([Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)). We therefore recommend: deploy the existing C++ particle filter plus alpha-beta, upgraded with StrangeFish2-style risk-weighted aggregation and an Obscuro-style sampled-infoset solver, with the recurrent network relegated to opponent-move likelihood and move-ordering; convert the PPO loop to MMD with a history-state critic V(h,s), λ near 1, legal-action masking, exact hidden-state reconstruction, and prioritised fictitious self-play over the frozen league; and build a batched C++ simulator, because 800 decisions/s is roughly 6× below a single-threaded Atari for-loop and two orders of magnitude below what one workstation reaches with batched C++ stepping ([EnvPool](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10558)). The evidence is thin exactly where it matters most: no published FoW or RBC agent has been trained end-to-end by self-play RL, no paper tests MMD or R-NaD on a chess-sized game, and the systems numbers are Atari and MuJoCo extrapolations. Throughout, sentences marked (inference) are our transfer to FoW chess and are claimed by no paper; everything else with a citation is a reported finding.

## Search engines, not learned policies, won every FoW and RBC contest

The deployment question is settled more firmly than the training question. Obscuro, by Zhang and Sandholm, is the first superhuman AI for chess.com Fog-of-War chess and it contains no learned policy or value: it keeps the full set of positions consistent with its observations (**average 17,264, at most about 10⁶**), samples a few hundred of them, builds a depth-limited tree in which the opponent's knowledge is modelled only to second order (2-KLUSS), solves it with Predictive CFR+ while a second thread expands the tree, evaluates leaves with Stockfish 14 at depth 11, and plays a purified mixed strategy over at most three actions ([Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2)). It beat the previous state of the art **85.1%** over 1,000 games, humans **97%** over 100, and the #1-ranked player **80%** over 20, running one CFR thread plus two expansion threads on a 6-core i5 at 3 minutes + 2 seconds ([Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2)). Its ablations rank what matters: dropping KLUSS re-optimisation costs most (full system scores **58.0%** against the frozen-knowledge variant), purification next (**70.2%**), one-sided tree growth least (**53.3%**), and replacing Stockfish with a "simple evaluation function" still scores **81.9%** against the previous SOTA, which the authors summarise as weak evaluation with good search remaining strong ([Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2)). The time-scaling curve is the single most useful number for a 2-core deployment: relative to 5 s/move, strength falls by roughly **−50 Elo at 1/16 s, −20 at 1/2 s, −8 at 4 s** ([Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2)). (Inference) A 2-thread machine at 180+2 clocks lands at a nominal 4–6 s per move; even if it is 4–8× slower per decision than Obscuro's setup, the curve puts the loss at 15–20 Elo against a system that is hundreds of Elo above human play.

The RBC record points the same way across three competitions. In 2019 StrangeFish won 22-bot round-robin with a winning record against every bot; in 2021 Fianchetto won with **91%** overall and Bayesian Elo 1759 to StrangeFish2's 1662; in 2022 StrangeFish2 won at 1762 with 68 more wins than second place ([RBC 2019](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v123/gardner20a/gardner20a.pdf); [RBC 2021](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf); [RBC 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf)). All three winners track an exhaustive or capped set of consistent boards, score (board, move) pairs with Stockfish or Lc0, and aggregate with a risk-weighted rule; StrangeFish2's source uses **70% weighted mean + 30% worst case**, random choice among moves within 10 centipawns, a **1,000-board tracking limit with a backlog** re-expanded down to 30, and a 250-board scoring sample, with Stockfish at depth 7 per evaluation ([StrangeFish2 source](https://github.com/ginoperrotta/reconchess-strangefish2)). Learned components placed but never won: Penumbra/Châteaux (ResNet-guided UCT over belief synopses) finished 3rd and 4th, Kevin (ResNet+LSTM imitation of StrangeFish2 logs as opponent model, Stockfish for expected value) 4th and 3rd, and a supervised-then-PPO-self-play agent with no search reached **Elo 1330, rank 27** on the public leaderboard ([Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810); [RBC 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf); [Bertram 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02029)). Penumbra's own ablation quantifies the value of search around a learned net: the full system rates 1747 against 1376 for the policy head alone, while removing static analysis made no statistically significant difference ([Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810)).

Two RBC findings transfer directly to how the existing alpha-beta should be used. First, the organisers' conclusion on information gathering hardened each year: minimising the number of possible states is "necessary but not sufficient", and StrangeFish2's decisive upgrade was choosing observations and moves "to maximize the expected outcome of the current turn", simulating the move it would make after each hypothetical observation ([RBC 2021](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf); [RBC 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf)). (Inference) In FoW chess vision is a by-product of piece placement rather than a separate sense action, so the analogue is a one-step lookahead over the observation partition each candidate move induces, costing O(|moves| × |sample|) per turn. Second, per-board best-move aggregation produces strategy fusion: with an attacking knight on d3 or f3, capturing on either square scores near zero under 50/50 weighting while the king retreat scores 0.99, a move that is best on neither board, so "it is not sufficient to only evaluate the absolute best moves, but rather … all possible moves" ([Bertram 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05864)). King danger is handled by every winner with hard constants rather than learned values: StrangeFish2 scores moving into check at **−7000**, remaining in check at −2000, king capture at +9000 ([StrangeFish2 source](https://github.com/ginoperrotta/reconchess-strangefish2)). The organisers also report a documented exploitability cycle among the 2022 top four (Fianchetto beat Kevin, Kevin beat Châteaux, Châteaux beat Fianchetto) and state that "a practical, sound (unexploitable) algorithm remains unknown" ([RBC 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf)). ReBeL is not a candidate: its authors say the belief-vector input "is intractable in games such as Recon Chess that have strategic depth but very little common knowledge" ([ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544)), and Obscuro measures common-knowledge sets in FoW chess at up to 10¹⁸ ([Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2)).

## Plain PPO self-play cycles; one reversed KL term makes it converge

The reason PPO self-play has worked for Go and chess but not for imperfect-information games is a property of the learning dynamics, not of function approximation. Perolat et al. generalise Poincaré recurrence from normal-form games to two-player zero-sum imperfect-information games: unregularised Follow-the-Regularized-Leader dynamics orbit the equilibrium ([Perolat 2021](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/perolat21a.html)). MMD states the same fact for gradient methods: with zero regularisation temperature, "projected gradient descent ascent … is known to diverge or cycle for any positive stepsize" ([MMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)). NFSP showed the older version of this failure with DQN in Leduc: the agent "learns a deterministic, greedy strategy" whose average "does not approach a Nash equilibrium either", because imperfect-information games "generally require stochastic strategies" ([NFSP](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01121)). DeepNash lists the usual ad-hoc stabilisers, training against past versions, reward shaping, and expert data, and says they "lack theoretical foundations, remain hard to tune and do not easily generalize" ([DeepNash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)). The concrete deep-RL datapoint is MMD Appendix J: PPO with RLlib defaults ran 10M steps of self-play in 3×3 Abrupt Dark Hex and Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe and ended with approximate exploitability **100±0 and 90±4 (×10⁻²)**, no better than a deterministic first-legal-action bot and worse than uniform random at 74 and 78 ([MMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)).

The fix shared by R-NaD and MMD is a KL-type pull toward a reference policy. R-NaD transforms the reward to r − η log(π^i(a)/π^i_reg(a)) + η log(π^{−i}(a^{−i})/π^{−i}_reg(a^{−i})), which gives the regularised game a unique fixed point with a Lyapunov function whose distance decreases exponentially; the fixed point is not yet a Nash equilibrium, so the reference is then replaced by the fixed point and the sequence of fixed points converges to Nash ([DeepNash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)). DeepNash used **η = 0.2** throughout, iteration lengths of 10k, 100k and 35k learner steps in three phases, a target-network EMA of 0.001 as the reference, and a smooth blend α_n = min(1, 2n/Δ_m) between old and new references ([DeepNash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378); [OpenSpiel R-NaD](https://github.com/google-deepmind/open_spiel/blob/v1.5/open_spiel/python/algorithms/rnad/rnad.py)). MMD does the same in the objective: π_{t+1} = argmax E_π q_t − α KL(π, ρ) − (1/η) KL(π, π_t), with closed form π_{t+1} ∝ [π_t ρ^{αη} e^{ηq_t}]^{1/(1+αη)}, and Theorem 3.4 gives linear last-iterate convergence to the regularised equilibrium when η ≤ α/L² ([MMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)). MMD's own comparison treats the two as siblings and argues it "only requires approximating bounded quantities" and reaches Leduc exploitability 0.08 in under 1,000 iterations versus F-FoReL's 200,000 ([MMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)).

What makes MMD the first thing to implement is that it is literally a PPO edit. Sokota et al. "implemented MMD as a modification of RLlib's PPO implementation by changing the adaptive forward KL regularization to a reverse KL regularization", with η_t = α_t = **0.05·sqrt(10⁷/t)** where t counts environment steps, and all other hyperparameters at default; the loss is E_t[(π/π_old)Â_t + αH(π) − β KL(π, π_old)] with β acting as an inverse stepsize, and a non-uniform magnet generalises the entropy term to −α KL(π, ρ) ([MMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)). After only 1M steps MMD's exploitability (34±2 / 37±1 ×10⁻²) was already below every NFSP or PPO seed at 10M; the moving-magnet variant ρ_{t+1} ∝ ρ_t^{1−η̃} π_{t+1}^{η̃} with α = 1, η = 0.1, η̃ = 0.05 converged faster than annealing in tabular tests ([MMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)). (Inference) The 0.05·sqrt(10M/t) schedule is a single reported working point, not a tuned recommendation, and those runs had no legal-action masking; the team should expect to sweep α over roughly a decade. R-NaD is the recipe proven at expert human level but is more invasive: the KL enters the reward, so GAE must run over transformed rewards, and DeepNash additionally uses NeuRD logit updates with a β = 2 band and full-episode unbootstrapped V-trace, choices the theory does not require ([DeepNash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)). (Inference) Start with MMD; fall back to R-NaD only if the MMD learner fails to reduce measured exploitability.

The league the team already has should be kept but repurposed as a sampling layer, not an equilibrium engine. AlphaStar's evidence is population-level and indirect: with exploiters the main-agent Elo rose from 1540 to 1824 and the relative population performance from 6% to 62%; prioritised fictitious self-play plus self-play held a 71% minimum win-rate against all past versions versus 46% for naive self-play; the league Nash "puts the most weight on recent players, suggesting that latest strategies largely dominate the previous ones, without much forgetting or cycling" ([AlphaStar](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). The sampling rule is f_hard(x) = (1−x)^p over estimated win probability, which the authors describe as "a form of smooth approximation of max-min optimisation" so that "strong but rare counter strategies" from exploiters cannot be ignored ([AlphaStar](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). (Inference) The structure to copy is periodic frozen snapshots, f_hard-weighted opponent sampling for roughly half the games, and one exploiter slot reset from the main agent; the constants (35/50/15, 70% thresholds, 2·10⁹-step snapshots) were tuned at 50k steps/s per learner. A real risk is that AlphaStar's diversity came from supervised initialisation, which a from-scratch policy lacks. No source tests a PFSP league on top of a regularised MMD or R-NaD learner, so whether the league's non-stationarity disturbs the fixed-point argument is untested.

## The near-chance value head is a diagnosable bias, not a mystery

Baisero & Amato give the cleanest explanation for a critic that will not learn. The POMDP policy-gradient theorem needs history values Q^π(h,a), and "replacing them with other state-based values will generally result in biased gradients"; Theorem 4.1 shows a time-invariant V^π(s) is generally ill-defined, and Theorem 4.2 shows that even when defined it is biased, because two histories with the same belief but different future behaviour have different V(h) and identical E_{s|h}[V(s)] ([Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)). The bias is worst "in tasks requiring information gathering, where the state alone does not generally indicate whether the agent has collected the necessary information", and even with unbiased Monte-Carlo targets the state critic's "target will continue shifting indefinitely" ([Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)). The fix is V^π(h,s), which satisfies V^π(h) = E_{s|h}[V^π(h,s)] for arbitrary problems and policies (Theorem 5.1), has variance governed by belief uncertainty and zero variance when the belief is deterministic, and in their gridworlds was "the only method capable of learning to solve the task at all" on Heaven-Hell while the state-only critic failed or collapsed ([Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)). AlphaStar confirms the upside of privileged information when it is used correctly: its value function sees both players' observations during training, and the ablation shows **82% versus 22%** average win-rate with and without opponent information ([AlphaStar](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). DeepNash, by contrast, used a non-privileged value head on a shared torso and relied on hand-built, policy-independent public-information planes ([DeepNash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)).

(Inference) In FoW chess the true board does not record what the agent has seen; two histories with identical boards can have very different beliefs, which is exactly the Theorem 4.2 construction, so a critic fed the full board is biased precisely where sensing matters, and a critic fed only the observation history is high-variance. The unbiased construction is a critic with its own GRU over the same observation stream, concatenated with the true board planes. Ni et al. supply the architectural half of that prescription: a shared encoder "increases the gradient norm and hinders learning" and "failed to learn either of these tasks", so actor and critic should have separate recurrent cores ([Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)). The same paper finds context length is "problem-specific", with 64 as the best default and full-episode context used for temporal-credit-assignment tasks ([Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)); (inference) FoW chess is a full-game-context problem, since early vision stays informative.

A second, independent explanation comes from VinePPO, and it matches the team's symptom exactly. In their PPO runs the value network reached explained variance 0.7–0.9 yet was "near chance" at identifying the best of five candidate next steps, and its error grew along the trajectory; swapping it for Monte-Carlo value estimates from K rollouts was the only change and produced up to 3× faster wall-clock convergence ([VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)). (Inference) VinePPO rollouts from the true simulator state, with the actor continuing from its own hidden state, are Monte-Carlo samples of exactly V(h,s), so by Theorem 5.1 they are an unbiased baseline without a learned critic; the obstacle is cost, since each probe is a full game against a stochastic opponent, and VinePPO already reports 2–5× PPO's per-iteration time in the deterministic LLM case. The practical use at 800 decisions/s is as a ground-truth diagnostic: sample a few hundred training states, estimate V(h,s) with 16–32 rollouts, and measure the learned critic's correlation and candidate-ranking accuracy, which is the protocol VinePPO used with 256-sample ground truth ([VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)).

The terminal ±1/0 reward also changes which PPO defaults apply. In the terminal-reward LLM regime RLOO found clipping active "< 5% of the time per batch", that removing clipping "does not impact learning meaningfully", and that reward decreases monotonically as λ drops below 1 ([RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)); VinePPO likewise settled on λ = 1, γ = 1 ([VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)). Engstrom et al. found code-level optimisations contribute "often significantly more than the choice of algorithm", that PPO-NoClip with those optimisations beats PPO without them, and that reward scaling is the most impactful trick ([Engstrom](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12729)); with a ±1 reward, scaling is moot. Value-loss clipping has no supporting evidence and hurt in Andrychowicz's sweep, per-minibatch advantage normalisation made no difference, and invalid-action masking was "critical" in Gym-μRTS ([37 details](https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/)). (Inference) Both λ=1 papers attribute tolerance of the extra variance to a strong pre-trained initialisation; a from-scratch chess policy over ~80 plies does not have that, so λ in 0.95–1.0 with the unbiased V(h,s) critic is the principled trade.

Recurrent mechanics are the last piece. R2D2's finding, cited second-hand because the primary PDF was inaccessible, is that zero-initialised hidden states cause the most severe recurrent-state staleness, burn-in alone helps only the start of a sequence, and the shipped configuration was stored states plus 40-step burn-in on 80-step sequences ([R2D2 via DI-engine](https://di-engine-docs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/12_policies/r2d2.html)). Recurrent-PPO grid searches found "longer sequences and greater hidden state sizes are favorable" ([Pleines 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11104)), and DeepNash's learner recomputes the exact network state by a forward pass over full episodes and processes ordered chunks in reverse so returns are exact ([DeepNash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)). (Inference) FoW games are short enough that full-prefix recomputation under current parameters, which has zero staleness by construction, is affordable; if not, a 40-step recomputed burn-in from the stored state is the evidence-backed fallback, and zero states mid-episode should never be used. ## The recommended stack, and where it can break

The stack has three layers, each justified by a different body of evidence. The deployment layer is the existing C++ particle filter plus alpha-beta, kept as the action selector and upgraded in the order Obscuro's and StrangeFish2's evidence ranks: evaluate every candidate move on every sampled board rather than taking per-board best moves, aggregate with a mean-plus-worst-case rule and hard king-danger constants, weight particles by an opponent-move likelihood, add the one-step expected-outcome-after-induced-observation term, randomise among near-equal moves, and, as the stretch goal, add a sampled-infoset CFR layer with alpha-beta leaves and a tree carried over between moves ([Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2); [StrangeFish2 source](https://github.com/ginoperrotta/reconchess-strangefish2)). The recurrent network is deployed through ONNX Runtime in the roles where RBC evidence shows learned models pay off on CPU: as the opponent-move model that sets particle weights (Kevin, JKU-CODA's Siamese weighting reached **52.9%** top-1 true-board accuracy versus 48.8% for a CNN) and as a move-ordering prior for the search ([RBC 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf); [Bertram 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05864)). The training layer is MMD on the existing PPO with the critic and recurrent fixes above and a light PFSP league. The infrastructure layer is a batched C++ simulator, discussed next.

(Inference) The specific risks, ordered by how much they could invalidate the plan:

1. Sample budget is unknown and probably large. MMD's deep runs were 1M–10M steps in games with a handful of decisions per episode; at 800 decisions/s a 10⁸-decision FoW run takes about 1.5 days and 10⁹ about two weeks, with no allowance for league games, best-response measurement, or sweeps ([MMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)). DeepNash consumed on the order of ≥10¹⁰ decisions on 1,024 TPU nodes ([DeepNash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)).
2. The regulariser constants are untuned. η = 0.2 (R-NaD) and 0.05·sqrt(10M/t) (MMD) are single working points; MMD's authors list adaptive schedules as future work ([MMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)).
3. League and regulariser interaction is untested in any source; a non-stationary opponent mixture may break the fixed-point argument.
4. From-scratch initialisation removes the diversity AlphaStar got from supervised play; exploration collapse to deterministic play is the NFSP failure mode and would be visible as rising exploitability.
5. The learned policy alone will be weak at arena time. The no-search RBC agent reached Elo 1330; Penumbra lost about 370 Elo without search ([Bertram 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02029); [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810)). The plan therefore never lets the network pick moves unaided.
6. Belief loss. A particle filter can lose the true state; Obscuro keeps the full set (avg 17k) and StrangeFish2 keeps a backlog to re-expand ([Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2); [StrangeFish2 source](https://github.com/ginoperrotta/reconchess-strangefish2)). The existing filter should back-stop with an exhaustive-or-backlog set whenever the count permits.
7. Two-core contention. ONNX Runtime defaults to one intra-op thread per physical core with spinning enabled, which will fight a search loop on 2 cores ([ONNX threading](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/performance/tune-performance/threading.html)); the forward-pass cost of a ~2M-parameter CNN+GRU on 2 cores has no published number and must be measured.
8. Memory. Obscuro's average 1.07M-node trees per move strain a 4 GiB limit; spend memory on tree retention, not particle count.
9. Strategy fusion and king danger. Per-board best-move voting is known to mis-handle attacks on the king; FoW rules (win by king capture, no check) make this the dominant tactical failure ([Bertram 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05864); [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2)).
10. Evidence quality. R2D2 facts are second-hand; Andrychowicz is cited via the 37-details blog; no RBC report gives per-bot hardware; Fianchetto has no paper or code.

## 800 decisions per second is the bottleneck, and a C++ simulator fixes it

The systems evidence makes the simulator decision easy. Python subprocess vectorisation "has extremely poor scalability with an almost flat curve"; a C++ thread-pool with batched asynchronous stepping gives **2.8× on a 12-core laptop** and 14.9–19.2× on 256 cores, and even a single environment in EnvPool gets "a free ~2x speedup" from removing per-step Python overhead ([EnvPool](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10558)). PufferLib measures the cliff directly: Gymnasium and SB3 multiprocessing "experience significant scaling degradation above 1000 synchronizations per second per core" ([PufferLib](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12905)). Sample Factory's asynchronous actor / GPU policy-worker / learner design reaches **75–85% of pure-simulation throughput** on a 36-core box with one RTX 2080 Ti, with double-buffered sampling that overlaps CPU stepping and GPU inference, and it found Python's multiprocessing queue itself consumed significant CPU above 10⁵ FPS ([Sample Factory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11751)). For reference, EnvPool's single-threaded Python for-loop reaches 4,893 Atari frames/s on a laptop ([EnvPool](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10558)); (inference) bitboard chess rules are cheaper than Atari emulation, so 800 decisions/s is almost certainly Python per-step overhead and inference round-trips, not game logic, and a batched C++ env exposing step(actions[N]) → obs[N] with GPU-batched, double-buffered inference should reach 2–5×10⁴ decisions/s on 8–12 cores and ~10⁵ on a 32-core workstation. These are extrapolations from Atari and MuJoCo; no throughput number for a batched chess or RBC environment exists in the literature.

The budget arithmetic shows why this matters more than any learner-side choice. KataGo reached superhuman Go with **241M search-backed samples over 19 days on ~27 V100s**, and its 6-block net was "Strong/Top Amateur" after 0.75 days ([KataGo](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)); AlphaZero's chess run used 44M games on 5,000 TPUs in 9 hours ([AlphaZero](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815)). The model-free reference class is worse: OpenAI Five used batches of 1–3M timesteps and 770 PFlops/s-days, and found batch-size speedup sublinear (2.5× from an 8× larger batch) with sample reuse near 1 ([OpenAI Five](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)). (Inference) At 2.9M decisions/hour the team matches KataGo's sample count in 3.5 days but each KataGo sample carries a 100–600-playout target, so the right comparison is the model-free systems at 10¹⁰–10¹² steps, three to four orders beyond a month at current speed. The lever on one GPU is wall-clock samples/s, not batch size, and synchronous PPO with a C++ env is within about 1.3× of IMPALA on the same CPUs ([IMPALA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01561)), so asynchrony is a second step, not a first.

(Inference) Invest in the C++ simulator, and do it before the MMD sweep rather than after. Every item in the training plan multiplies simulation cost: league games, best-response exploitability runs, VinePPO ground-truth probes, and α sweeps. A 25× throughput gain converts a two-week 10⁹-decision run into half a day and makes K-rollout Monte-Carlo baselines affordable as a training signal rather than only as a diagnostic. The existing C++ belief and alpha-beta code already contains the move generator, which lowers the cost of a batched env considerably. Keep the design synchronous and persistent (finished games reset in place) at first, since variable-length games are exactly the high-variance-step case where lockstep batching stalls on the slowest game ([IMPALA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01561)).

## Conclusion

The literature reverses the instinct that a stronger learned policy is the path to a stronger bot. In games with hidden actions and little common knowledge, the systems that win are those that reason over a sampled belief at decision time and treat learned models as priors and likelihoods; the team's existing C++ belief and search stack is already the right architecture, and the fastest Elo gains will come from evaluating every move on every sampled board, aggregating with explicit risk weights, and mixing deliberately, not from a better GRU. The deep-RL contribution is real but narrower than it looks: an unbiased privileged critic, a regularised self-play objective that converges instead of cycling, and a league that prevents forgetting together give a policy whose honest job is to tell the search what the opponent is likely to do.

What the evidence cannot yet say is how many decisions FoW chess needs under MMD, whether a PFSP league coexists with a regularised fixed point, and what a 2M-parameter network costs per forward on 2 cores. Those three unknowns are exactly what Experiments 2, 3 and 4 measure, and the C++ simulator is what makes the first two measurable in days rather than months. If the MMD curve bends down and the upgraded search beats the current bot, the project has both a sound learner and a deployable engine; if the learner stalls, the search still ships.
