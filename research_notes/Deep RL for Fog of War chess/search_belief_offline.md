# Search-based and offline methods for imperfect-information games, as they bear on a 2-core Fog of War chess bot

Scope: ReBeL (Brown et al. 2020), Obscuro (Zhang & Sandholm 2025) and its predecessor KLSS (Zhang & Sandholm 2021), IQL (Kostrikov et al. 2021) and CQL (Kumar et al. 2020), and the Reconnaissance Blind Chess (RBC) competition literature 2019/2021/2022 plus the strongest published bots (StrangeFish/StrangeFish2, Penumbra/Châteaux (DSMCP), Fianchetto, Kevin, JKU-CODA/Siamese weighting). Deployment target kept in mind throughout: 2 CPU cores, 4 GiB, no GPU, no network, 15 s init, 180 s + 2 s/move clocks, existing C++ particle-filter belief + alpha-beta engine + recurrent neural policy.

Naming correction for the brief: Penumbra (and its successor Châteaux) is by Gregory Clark (Google / ML Collective), not Bernardoni. William Bernardoni authored the 2019 bot "wbernar5" and the open-source "Baseline-Bots" repo that Penumbra was benchmarked against — [Clark 2021, arXiv 2110.01810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810); [Gardner et al. 2020, PMLR 123, Table 1](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v123/gardner20a/gardner20a.pdf).

## Key Question 1 — ReBeL: public belief state, search on it, test-time cost, and feasibility on 2 CPU cores in ~4 s/move

### Takeaway
ReBeL searches over a *public belief state* (public history + a common-knowledge probability vector over every player's possible private infostates) using depth-limited CFR-D with a learned PBS value network; it is sound only when the number of infostates per public state is small enough to enumerate as a network input (2×1326 hands in poker). The authors themselves say this is intractable for Recon-chess-like games with little common knowledge, and FoW chess is in that class, so ReBeL as published is not a candidate for the 2-core bot; only its ideas (value-of-belief leaf evaluation, random-iteration sampling for unexploitable mixing) transfer.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: a PBS is β = (Δ S₁(s_pub), …, Δ S_N(s_pub)), "a joint probability distribution over the agents' possible infostates" conditioned on the public state; the construction turns an imperfect-information game into "a continuous-state perfect-information game" where "the probability that each player is holding each private card is common knowledge among all players" (Section 4) — [ReBeL, arXiv 2007.13544](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Search: a depth-limited subgame rooted at the current PBS is solved with CFR-D for T iterations; leaf PBS values are set from the value network, "the value of every discrete representation leaf node z is set to v̂(sᵢ(z)|β^πᵗ_z)"; in poker "our agent always solves to the end of the current betting round" (Section 5.1, Section 8) — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Test-time policy: ReBeL does not play the average strategy; it samples "a random iteration t ∼ unif{t_warm+1, T}" and plays the policy of that iteration; Theorem 3 states that with a trained value net, Algorithm 1 at test time "will approximate a Nash equilibrium", i.e. "we play a Nash equilibrium policy in expectation" even if the opponent knows the algorithm (Sections 5–7) — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Network input for HUNL: "1 (agent index) + 1 (acting agent) + 1 (pot) + 5 (board) + 2×1326 (infostate beliefs)" — the belief vector over all 1326 hole-card combinations for each player (Appendix on HUNL setup) — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Training compute: "a single machine for training and up to 128 machines with 8 GPUs each for data generation"; for full HUNL "90 DGX-1 machines, each with 8 × 32GB Nvidia V100 GPUs for data generation" — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Test-time cost (poker): "ReBeL played faster than 2 seconds per hand and never needed more than 5 seconds for a decision" (Section 8). The number of GPUs used at test time and the value of T for HUNL were not located in the extracted text (see Gaps) — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Liar's Dice: "we use single GPU for training and 60 CPU threads for data generation"; "during both training and evaluation we run the search algorithm for 1024 iterations" (Appendix E). Exploitability with depth-limited CFR-D search was reported in Table 2; the two extraction passes returned differing numbers for the rows (0.041/0.020 vs 0.017/0.015/0.024/0.017 for the 1×4f, 1×5f, 1×6f, 2×3f variants), versus 0.001–0.002 for full-game tabular CFR, so treat the exact row values as unverified but the order of magnitude (≈10× worse than full-game CFR) as reliable — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Results (Table 1): vs top human Dong Kim "165 ± 69" mbb/g over 7,500 hands (variance-reduced); vs Slumbot "45 ± 5" mbb/g; vs BabyTartanian8 "9 ± 4" mbb/g. Turn-endgame hold'em: ReBeL's exploitability was "equivalent to running about 125 iterations of full-game tabular CFR" (Figure 2) — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Search-algorithm choice: "flexible with respect to the choice of search algorithm"; CFR-D used for the main poker runs; fictitious-play variant (FLOP) in Appendix H; CFR-AVG vs CFR-D comparison appears in the appendix with CFR-AVG winning by "9 ± 4" and CFR-D beating Slumbot by "39 ± 6" in one reported pairing (extraction was ambiguous about which opponent each refers to) — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Stated limitation (Section 9, verbatim): "the input to its value and policy functions currently grows linearly with the number of infostates in a public state. This is intractable in games such as Recon Chess that have strategic depth but very little common knowledge." Theoretical guarantees are for "two-player zero-sum games" only — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Independent corroboration: the RBC 2021 organisers write that PBS decomposition and PBS-value networks (citing Brown et al. 2020) "can yield strong play for Texas hold-em poker, in which the only non-public information is each player's private hand. In RBC, however, there is much more private information, and thus public subgames often include a tremendous number of states" (Section 3) — [Perrotta et al. 2022, PMLR 176](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf). The 2022 report repeats: "Because RBC has little public information, these approaches do not apply well to RBC. (Each public state would include a large portion of the game.)" — [Gardner et al. 2023, PMLR 220](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- Clark (Penumbra) likewise: ReBeL "relies on public knowledge and fails to scale to games with hidden actions and substantial private information, such as RBC" (Section 2) — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810).
- Obscuro quantifies the problem for FoW chess specifically: "Common-knowledge sets can have size 10¹⁸—far too large to be enumerated"; individual infosets reach 10⁶–10⁹ nodes, whereas in poker common-knowledge sets "have size at most … ≈ 1.6×10⁶, and can thus easily be held in memory" (Section 1–2) — [Obscuro, arXiv 2506.01242v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).

### Inferences
- (Inference) In FoW chess the public state after move 1 is essentially "whose turn it is plus the capture/time history", so a ReBeL-style PBS would have to carry a distribution over ~10⁶–10⁹ positions per player and, worse, the joint over both players' infosets. No fixed-width network input can encode that; the authors' own Section 9 caveat is decisive. ReBeL is therefore not deployable in its published form regardless of CPU budget.
- (Inference) The transferable pieces are (a) a learned *belief-conditioned* value used at search leaves, which in our setting would be v(particle set summary) rather than v(PBS); and (b) the random-iteration trick, which gives a principled mixed policy at test time without storing averages. Both are cheap at inference; the expensive part of ReBeL is the equilibrium-search loop, not the networks.
- (Inference) On the stated ~4 s/move budget on 2 cores, even depth-limited CFR over a few hundred sampled positions is what Obscuro does (Q2), and that is the realistic ceiling; ReBeL's "end of betting round" subgames have no analogue, because FoW chess has no public-information checkpoints.

### Gaps
- The exact T (CFR iterations per subgame) and test-time GPU count for HUNL were not found in the extracted HTML (first 100k chars + Appendices G–J); the relevant appendix section (HUNL hyperparameters) was not returned by the fetch tool. Do not quote a number.
- The Liar's Dice exploitability rows were returned inconsistently by two extraction passes; use the PDF Table 2 directly before citing specific values.

## Key Question 2 — Obscuro: problem, algorithm, games/results, and search without a public belief state

### Takeaway
Obscuro (Zhang & Sandholm, 2025) is the first superhuman AI for exactly the user's game — chess.com Fog of War chess — and it does it with pure online search on a CPU: it keeps the full set P of positions consistent with its own observations (avg ≈17k, ≤10⁶), samples a few hundred of them, builds a depth-limited game tree where the opponent's knowledge is modelled only to second order (2-KLUSS), solves it with PCFR+ while a second thread expands the tree (one-sided growing-tree CFR), evaluates leaves with Stockfish 14 at depth 11, and plays a purified mixed strategy. It beat the previous SOTA 85.1%, beat humans 97%, and beat the #1 ranked player 80%, running 3 threads on a 6-core i5 at 3+2; its time-scaling curve shows only ~−50 Elo at 1/16 s/move relative to 5 s/move, which is the strongest evidence in this survey that a belief-sampled search is feasible on 2 cores at a few seconds per move.

### Cited Findings
- Title/authors: "General search techniques without common knowledge for imperfect-information games, and application to superhuman Fog of War chess", Brian Hu Zhang (MIT CSAIL) and Tuomas Sandholm (CMU) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Game: FoW rules as used: "A player wins by capturing the opposing king. There is no check or checkmate"; "After every move, each player observes all squares onto which her pieces can legally move"; FoW "has surged in popularity due to its implementation on the major chess website chess.com" while RBC "is not played competitively by humans" (Section 2) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Problem statement: "Most algorithms for subgame solving … rely on the ability to reason about common knowledge, or often even the ability to enumerate the entire common-knowledge set"; in FoW chess common-knowledge sets reach 10¹⁸ (Section 1) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Belief representation: "The program maintains the full set P of possible positions … Although P is small enough to fit in memory (usually |P| ≤ 10⁶), it is too large to feasibly allow nontrivial reasoning about every single position"; "The program instead samples a small subset I ⊆ P at random, whose size is no more than a few hundred positions" (Section 3). Implementation: MinInfosetSize = 256 (Figure 9); when the carried-over tree has too few root nodes, states are added "sampling at random without replacement from the set of possible states"; P is updated "simply enumerating all possibilities" consistent with observations (Appendix C.1) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Measured sizes (Appendix D, 1,000-game sample at 5 s/move): "Average number of possible positions: 17,264"; "Average search tree size: 1,070,552 nodes, 14,404 infosets"; "Average search tree size carried over from previous search: 181,421 nodes, 3,162 infosets" — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Algorithm loop (Section 3): (1) build subgame with knowledge-limited unfrozen subgame solving (KLUSS), incorporating the saved partial tree; (2) run Predictive CFR+ (PCFR+) to get strategies (xᵗ, yᵗ); (3) expand the tree with one-sided growing-tree CFR using PUCT-style node selection; (4) repeat 2–3 until time is up; (5) act by sampling from a purified strategy that mixes among ≤3 actions — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- How common knowledge is avoided: KLUSS "address[es] the issue of reasoning about common knowledge by assuming that sufficiently high-order knowledge is essentially irrelevant to game play"; nodes are pruned when "everyone knows that everyone knows … the true node is not u". Obscuro uses 2-KLUSS, in which nodes in Ī²∖Ī are "unfrozen and hence re-optimized in the subgame" rather than frozen as in ZS21's 1-KLSS; at newly added nodes "the opponent is assumed to have perfect information, and the alternate value is set to min{ṽ(h), v*}" (Sections 3.1, C.1, C.6) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- One-sided GT-CFR (Section C.4): "Fix one player to be the exploring player … The non-exploring player will play according to its current strategy … The exploring player will play a perturbed version"; the expanding player alternates after every node expansion; Theorem 1 proves convergence to an approximate Nash equilibrium — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Leaf evaluation: "Stockfish 14 in MultiPV mode, at depth 11 on node z, which gives evaluations for all children of z in a single call", clamped to [−1, +1]; this "reduces the overhead of calling Stockfish to one call per node being expanded" (Section C.5) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Threads/hardware: "Obscuro, for its human matches, ran on a single desktop machine with a 6-core Intel i5 CPU"; "one thread running CFR and two threads expanding the game tree, which is shared across all three threads"; the CFR thread "uses no locks". Human matches at "3 minutes + 2 seconds per move"; ablations at 5 s/move "on AMD EPYC 64-core server using 10 cores" (Section 4, Appendix D) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Results: vs prior SOTA ZS21, 1,000 games, "85.1% (+834 =33 −133)"; vs 100 human games (ratings 1450–2006) "97% (+97 =0 −3)"; vs the #1 ranked player (2318, Aug 2024) 20 games "80% (+16 =0 −4)", p = 0.0118 (Section 4) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Ablations (Section 4.1; score of full Obscuro vs the variant): purification off → 70.2% (1,000 games); KLUSS off (1-KLSS used) → 58.0%; one-sided GT-CFR off (two-sided) → 53.3% (10,000 games); non-uniform resolve-root distribution off → 53.3%; and Obscuro with a "simple evaluation function" instead of Stockfish still scored 81.9% (+787 =63 −150) vs ZS21, i.e. "weak eval with good search still strong" — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Time scaling (Figure 3, Elo relative to Obscuro at 5 s/move): 1/16 s ≈ −50; 1/8 s ≈ −40; 1/4 s ≈ −30; 1/2 s ≈ −20; 1 s ≈ −15; 2 s ≈ −12; 4 s ≈ −8; 8 s ≈ −3; 16 s ≈ +2; the paper describes diminishing returns and says moving from 1/16 s to 16 s "approximately doubles playing strength" relative to 5 s baseline (Section 4.2) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Mixed strategies are necessary: "if a player plays a pure strategy that the opponent knows, the opponent would essentially be playing regular chess"; example after 1. c4 d5, mixing 2. Qa4 (low prob.) with 2. Nc3 wins "almost a full pawn (in expectation)" (Appendix E.2). Purification: "We only allow mixing among actions other than the highest-probability action if they have appeared continuously in the support of xᵗ for every iteration t > T₁/₂" (C.8) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Equilibrium vs exploitation: Obscuro "does not know or attempt to model the opponent. It will simply play what it believes to be a near-equilibrium strategy" (E.6) — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Risk-taking / king exposure (E.5): from a losing position Obscuro played "the highly risky queen maneuver Qg8-g1-a1-a7-a8, leaving its king exposed but eventually winning"; "This sequence of moves heavily exploits the opponent's imperfect information" — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Limitations stated: (Section 5) "Obscuro stores at all times the entire set of possible states in memory. While these techniques were feasible for FoW chess—due to the similarity to regular chess and the relatively small infosets—one can imagine even more complex games on which they will not work directly"; (C.6) "Like 1-KLSS, 2-KLUSS lacks safety guarantees in the worst case"; (C.7) last-iterate rather than average-iterate PCFR+ is used, justified because "the typical Nash gap was approximately equivalent to half a pawn"; (3.2) PCFR+ is only guaranteed to converge in average strategies — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- First-mover statistics from self-play (Appendix): "White scored 57.5% (+4935 =1623 −3442)" across 10,000 games — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- Predecessor ZS21 ("Subgame solving without common knowledge", NeurIPS 2021) introduced KLSS and claims "the first strong AI for the challenge problem dark chess"; it used LP-based equilibrium finding and strict 1-KLSS; the base method "can increase exploitability compared to the blueprint strategy" but "three avenues by which safety can be guaranteed" are given — [Zhang & Sandholm 2021, arXiv 2106.06068](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06068); [Obscuro C.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2). The RBC organisers note 1-KLSS "was used to play dark chess, although not at superhuman level" — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf).

### Inferences
- (Inference) Obscuro is the closest published system to the user's target and demonstrates that *no learned value or policy is required* for superhuman FoW play; search quality dominates (the "simple eval" ablation still scores 81.9% vs ZS21). For a 2-core bot, the ranking of investments implied by the ablations is: KLUSS-style subgame construction with re-optimisation (+27 pts vs frozen) > purification (+20) > one-sided expansion (+3).
- (Inference) Compute budget mapping: the user's 180 s + 2 s/move at ~40 moves ≈ 6.5 s/move nominal; Obscuro's human matches ran 3 threads on a 6-core i5 at 3+2 (≈ similar nominal time); the time-scaling curve implies that even a 4–8× slower machine (2 threads instead of 3, lower per-core speed) lands around the 1/2–1 s/move point, i.e. ≈ −15 to −20 Elo relative to the published system. That is a small loss relative to the gap Obscuro shows over humans.
- (Inference) Memory is the real risk: 1.07M nodes / 14k infosets per move plus Stockfish's hash within 4 GiB is tight but plausible if node structs are compact; the carried-over tree (181k nodes) is what makes incremental re-solving cheap, so the 4 GiB budget should be spent on tree retention rather than a bigger particle set.
- (Inference) The paper's belief set is *exhaustive* (full P), not a particle filter; the user's particle-filter belief corresponds to Obscuro's sample I (256+). The gap to Obscuro is that P is needed to (i) guarantee the true state is in the sample's support and (ii) resample without replacement; a particle filter with resampling from a stored exhaustive-or-backlogged set (StrangeFish2's BoardSetBacklog, Q3) is the pragmatic middle ground.
- (Inference) Obscuro says nothing about explicit information gathering because FoW vision is a by-product of piece placement; information value is captured implicitly through the equilibrium computation over sampled infosets. A one-ply "expected value after observation" term (StrangeFish2's sense objective) is the cheap heuristic substitute if full subgame solving is not affordable.

### Gaps
- Obscuro does not report CFR iterations per move, Stockfish calls per move, or peak memory; only average tree sizes.
- No published Obscuro ablation isolates "alpha-beta leaf evaluation at depth d" vs Stockfish depth 11; the "simple evaluation function" ablation is the only proxy and its definition was not extracted.
- Code for Obscuro is not referenced as open-source in the extracted text; no repository found.

## Key Question 3 — RBC competitions: what winning bots did, belief sizes, learned entries, and evidence on king danger and information gathering

### Takeaway
Across 2019/2021/2022, every top RBC bot tracked an (ideally exhaustive) set of opponent-consistent boards, weighted them (Stockfish-based or learned), evaluated (board, move) pairs with Stockfish or Lc0, and chose moves by a risk-weighted aggregate (StrangeFish2: 70% weighted mean + 30% worst-case, random choice within 10 cp); the decisive upgrades were in *sensing policy* (choose the observation that maximises expected outcome of this turn's move, not the one that minimises state count) and in prioritised sub-sampling when belief sets blow up. Learned components placed but never won: Penumbra/Châteaux (ResNet-guided UCT over synopses) 3rd/4th, Kevin (ResNet+LSTM imitation of StrangeFish2 logs + Stockfish EV) 4th/3rd, a PPO self-play no-search agent reached only Elo 1330 (rank 27), and the Siamese-weighting JKU-CODA 8th. King-danger handling in the winners is explicit and heavy-handed (into-check −7000 cp, remain-in-check −2000, king capture +9000, check-detecting senses), and the organisers' own analysis says minimising raw uncertainty is necessary but not sufficient.

### Cited Findings

**Competition structure, time controls, outcomes**
- 2019: 22 bots, 12 round-robin rounds, 5,544 games, "Each player had a 15 minute time limit to make all their moves"; StrangeFish won with "20 more total wins than the second-place bot and a winning record against every bot" (464-40 overall per the search summary) — [Gardner et al. 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v123/gardner20a/gardner20a.pdf); [PMLR page](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v123/gardner20a.html).
- 2021: 18 bots, 30 rounds, 9,180 games, "15 minutes of cumulative time per bot per game", bots "required to support four simultaneous games"; Fianchetto won "with a 91% overall win rate and at least a 66% win rate against every opponent"; Bayesian Elo: Fianchetto 1759, StrangeFish2 1662, penumbra 1584, Kevin 1544, Oracle 1503, Gnash 1454 — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf).
- 2022: 18 bots, 60 games per pair, 9,180 games; "15 minutes per game plus 5 seconds per turn"; draw after 50 captureless/pawnless moves; StrangeFish2 won "with 68 total wins more than the bot with the next greatest number and a winning record against every other bot"; Elo: StrangeFish2 1762, Fianchetto 1644, Kevin 1623, Châteaux 1621, ROOKie 1551, Oracle 1465, Marmot 1329, JKU-CODA 1283 — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- Organisers' verdict every year: "none of the algorithms converge to an optimal, unexploitable strategy" (2021 abstract); "a practical, sound (unexploitable) algorithm remains unknown" (2022 abstract) — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf); [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- Exploitability cycle observed in 2022: "Fianchetto performed better against Kevin, Kevin performed better against Châteaux, and Châteaux performed better against Fianchetto … this cycle in performance illustrates the exploitability one can have in a seemingly strong algorithm by an adaptive opponent" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).

**StrangeFish (2019 winner) — decision rules from the paper and source**
- Paper description: "Tracks all possible board states. Evaluates players' relative advantage on any board state using Stockfish plus custom heuristics … Uses the calculated advantage scores (1) to estimate the probability of a hypothetical board being the true state, (2) as a proxy for game rollouts … (3) to assess sensing importance by score disparity in move choices across the remaining hypothetical board states. Chooses moves to maximize the change in position advantage averaged across a random sample of possible board states. Chooses sense locations to maximize expected influence on the following move decision" (Table 1) — [Gardner et al. 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v123/gardner20a/gardner20a.pdf).
- Source constants (multiprocessing_strategies.py): move score = `mean_score_factor 0.9` + `min_score_factor 0.1` + `max_score_factor 0.0`; `threshold_score_factor 0.01`; board probability weight = logistic `1/(1+exp(−2 ln3 / board_weight_90th_percentile × score))` with `board_weight_90th_percentile 5000` cp; sample size = `time_for_phase / time_per_board`; `num_workers 2`; `turns_to_plan_for 7`, `min_time_for_turn 1.0`, sense/move time split 0.8/0.2; `checkmate_sense_override True` (when every board scores as into_check, sense the square that minimises the variance of the opponent king's location); `sense_by_move` bonus for moves that reduce the board set; `force_promotion_queen` — [StrangeFish repo](https://github.com/ginop/reconchess-strangefish) (file strangefish/strategies/multiprocessing_strategies.py).
- README: "Expansion and filtering of the board set are parallelized, and set expansion is begun during the opponent's turn"; move scores in the tournament "were 90% mean and 10% min"; sensing "calculates move scores for the full board set (or for as large of a random sample as time allows) and compares those to scores calculated for each sub-set of boards that a sensing result would produce" — [StrangeFish README](https://github.com/ginop/reconchess-strangefish).

**StrangeFish2 (2021 runner-up, 2022 winner) — decision rules from the paper and source**
- 2021 report: "sense actions are selected by estimating probabilities for all possible observations, choosing a move following each of those hypothetical observations, and estimating probabilities for all possible outcomes of that move. These probabilities are based on the estimated distribution over board states, which is a function of the opponent's strength in each position" — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf).
- 2022 report: "Both sense and move decisions are made to maximize the expected outcome of the current turn. Improved performance was driven by developments in board state value heuristics – which continue to augment Stockfish engine analysis – and by prioritized sub-sampling of tracked positions when time limits did not permit evaluation of all hypotheses" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- Source docstring: "I used 70% average + 30% minimum score to evaluate move options"; the sense objective is "primarily the same objective as the move strategy: the expected outcome of the current turn", plus the 2019 "expected change in move evaluation" term, plus a set-size term that "is typically small, but if the set size grows large it can dominate the sense decision. In that case, StrangeFish2 saves considerable computational effort by only computing this term". Move-evaluation sampling 2022: "samples a move similar to UCT (highest score plus bonus for under-visited options) and then samples a board for which that move has not been evaluated … which greatly reduced the occurrence of moves selected without being evaluated on the true board" — [StrangeFish2 source, strangefish_strategy.py](https://github.com/ginoperrotta/reconchess-strangefish2).
- Source constants: `BOARD_SET_LIMIT = 1_000` ("number of boards in set at which we stop processing and store for later"), `REPOPULATE_BOARD_SET_TARGET = 30`; `SENSE_SAMPLE_LIMIT = 2500`, `SCORE_SAMPLE_LIMIT = 250`; `swap_sense_size = 10_000`, `swap_sense_min_size = 150`, `swap_sense_time = 90` s (switch from max-outcome sensing to min-states sensing when too many unscored boards or low time); MoveConfig `mean 0.7 / min 0.3 / max 0.0`, `threshold_score 10` cp (random choice among moves within 10 cp of best, "Deterministic move patterns are reduced"), `sampling_exploration_coef 1000`, `move_sample_rep_limit 100`; SenseConfig `expected_outcome_coef 1.0`, `worst_outcome_coef 0.2`, `outcome_variance_coef −0.3`, `score_variance_coef 0.15`, `boards_per_centipawn 50`; TimeConfig `turns_to_plan_for 16`, `min_time_for_turn 3.0`, `max_time_for_turn 40.0`, sense/move split 0.7/0.3, `calc_time_per_move 0.005` s initial estimate; board weight logistic with `board_weight_90th_percentile 3000` cp and `min_board_weight 0.02`; when exactly one board remains, 90% of the time it plays the engine move with a 2.0 s Stockfish limit; dominated sense squares are pruned by partition comparison — [StrangeFish2 source](https://github.com/ginoperrotta/reconchess-strangefish2) (strangefish_strategy.py, strangefish_mht_core.py).
- King/check heuristics (ScoreConfig in utilities/rbc_move_score.py): `capture_king_score 9000`, `checkmate_score 8000`, `points_per_move_to_mate 0.95`, `into_check_score −7000`, `remain_in_check_penalty −2000`, `op_into_check_score −4000`, `search_depth 7` (Stockfish ply per (board, move) evaluation), `reward_attacker 20`, `reward_check_threats 15`, `require_sneak True` ("Only reward bonus points to aggressive moves if they are sneaky (aren't captures)"), `unsafe_attacker_penalty −15`, `hanging_material_penalty_ratio 0.1`, `capture_penalty −5`, `passing_reward 5`, `castling_rights_bonus 15`, `use_absolute_score 0.85` — [StrangeFish2 source](https://github.com/ginoperrotta/reconchess-strangefish2) (strangefish/utilities/rbc_move_score.py).

**Fianchetto (2021 winner, 2022 runner-up)**
- "Built using the source code of StrangeFish … For board evaluations, Fianchetto uses Lc0 rather than Stockfish. Lc0 speeds up the evaluation step by giving scores to all possible actions through a single forward pass of a neural network, with the additional option of evaluating multiple boards in parallel on a GPU. The gain in speed enables the expansion of the search tree to include a probabilistic model of opponent behavior, itself also taken from Lc0. Fianchetto maintains a belief over all possible board states, which is recomputed on each turn using the POMDP belief update equation" — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf).
- 2022 changes: "an increased weight to defensive sensing and moving to detect opponent attacking moves that have been given a low probability. These weights are opponent-specific, prioritizing more defensive play against more aggressive opponents. Additionally, chooses its first move from an opening book, designed to give preference to closed chess positions, with the rationale that closed positions tend to have lower uncertainty and smaller information set sizes" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- No standalone Fianchetto paper or public repository was found; the competition reports are the only descriptions (searched "Fianchetto reconnaissance blind chess Kalyanakrishnan Taufeeque").

**Penumbra / Châteaux (DSMCP; learned, search-based)**
- 2019: placed 18th, "Underperformed in the tournament due to hardware issues"; design: tracks all opponent boards, Gibbs-samples states, fixed-length playouts, UCB bandit, "ten block residual network which was trained on historical RBC games" — [Gardner et al. 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v123/gardner20a/gardner20a.pdf). Won the 2020 online leaderboard "versus 33 other programs"; 3rd in 2021; as Châteaux 4th in 2022 — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810); [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf); [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- Method: "constructs a belief state with an unweighted particle filter and plans via playouts that start at samples drawn from the belief state"; "seeks a best-response strategy rather than a Nash equilibrium strategy"; treats "playing against specific opponents as related offline reinforcement learning (RL) problems and exploits predictability"; belief particles are *sets of boards from the opponent's perspective* ("second order" belief) subsampled to ℓ = 128 states and summarised by a permutation-invariant "synopsis" of 104 bitboards (AND/OR over the set) fed to a 10-residual-block, 128-filter ResNet with 14 opponent-specific headsets (policy, value, SoonWin, SoonLose, PieceCount heads) — [Clark 2021, Sections 1, 5, 5.1, 5.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810).
- Training data: 85.6k games / 16.8M actions ("All" headset), Top-1 action accuracy 33.6% (All) to 50.0% (vs Dyn.Entropy); "Training and evaluation were run on four RTX 2080 Ti GPUs"; "Inference is done in batches of 256 during both training and online planning"; "The program processes approximately 4,000 nodes per second, and it plays randomly when the number of possible states exceeds 9 million" (Section 5.3–5.4, Table 1) — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810).
- Ablation (Table 2, BayesElo anchored at StockyInference = 1500): PenumbraMixture (m=1) 1747±11; PenumbraCache (policy-only playouts) 1727±10; PenumbraTree (pure UCT) 1641±9; PenumbraNetwork (no playouts, policy head only) 1376±9; "removing the search algorithm is detrimental while the effect of removing the static analysis is not statistically significant"; "modeling the opponent with a stronger policy outperformed modeling it more accurately" (Section 6) — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810).
- Caution/paranoia grid (Table 3): best cell 1726±19 at caution 20% / paranoia 10% vs 1711±19 baseline; differences within CIs; paranoia 30% drops to ~1666–1691 — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810).
- Risk evidence: "Playing RBC well requires balancing risks and rewards" — Figure 2 shows the white queen sent to g8 surviving because the opponent could only localise it to "one of 25 squares", and the mirror case where the queen was detected and captured; Figure 1: over 98k historical games "the win percentage tends to decrease as the number of possible states increases"; "The median of the maximum number of possible states encountered during a game is 4,869" — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810).
- Static analysis: "Penumbra can detect some forced wins in the sense phase, the move phase, and during the opponent's turn"; playouts terminate as soon as a player could win (Section 5.4) — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810).
- Châteaux 2022: network "trained on approximately 600,000 games available online"; "maintains a 'second-order' belief state of the opponent's information with an unweighted particle filter made up of possible approximate information states from the opponent's perspective" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).

**Kevin (learned opponent model + Stockfish EV; 4th 2021, 3rd 2022)**
- 2021: "keeps track of all possible board states and a probability distribution over them. It chooses sense actions to minimize the expected difference in value between its next move and its theoretical best move for the true state. Each opponent turn, it calculates the probability distribution over board states by performing depth-limited CFR to approximate an opponent's Nash equilibrium strategy. The CFR uses unsafe subgame solving (Ganzfried and Sandholm, 2015) over a sampled subset of possible board states, and it uses Stockfish to evaluate leaf nodes. For its own move, the bot maximizes its expected win probability" — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf).
- 2022: "Explicitly keeps track of a distribution over possible game histories. (Opponent senses matter, not only board state.) Predicts opponent actions (both senses and moves) using a neural network. The neural network is trained with imitation learning on game logs of StrangeFish2. The neural net inputs come from OpenSpiel and its RBC implementation, and they pass through a residual neural network (ResNet) and a long short-term memory (LSTM) network, which output a distribution over legal actions. Selects the best move by maximizing expected value over the distribution of histories, using Stockfish to evaluate board states" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).

**Other learned / neural entries**
- JKU-CODA (8th, 2022): contextual preference ranking with a Siamese network over boards and observations to weight the information set; "Evaluates all possible moves on the most likely boards with Stockfish and chooses the move with the best combination of worst-case and weighted-average evaluation" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf). Journal version: 582,450 games, information sets capped at 5,000 boards, 27M triplets, trained 22 epochs / 61 h on one A100; Siamese top-1 true-board accuracy 52.91% vs CNN 48.82%, vs StrangeFish2's internal weighting and Stockfish ranking (both much lower; average info-set size 1,100); the agent weights only the top-100 likeliest boards for sensing; temperature t=10 best; best player "ranked 5th on the public leaderboard" — [Bertram et al. 2024, arXiv 2407.05864](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05864).
- Bertram et al. 2022 (no search; supervised from public logs then PPO self-play): "an ELO of 1330 on the RBC leaderboard, which places our agent at position 27"; the agent "plays acceptably well without search and without making assumptions about the true game state" — [Bertram, Fürnkranz, Müller 2022, arXiv 2208.02029](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02029).
- uccchess (14th, 2022): StrangeFish with "a set of 20 neural networks to replace the chess engine", opponent class chosen by an RL-trained classifier; rating "significantly decreased by forfeits" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- GarrisonNRL (11th/13th): "Maintains a sample of up to 200 possible boards"; when below 50 boards uses Lc0 with the Maia-1700 network per board and "the most common best move"; otherwise single-observer ISMCTS — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf); [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- 2019 CFR-based bots: "genetic" (4th) built a 3–4 level extensive-form tree from possible states with a per-player uncertainty term and solved it with CFR; "Marmot" (6th) used MC-CFR with online outcome sampling from a finite past horizon; the organisers note Marmot and genetic "outperformed their competition rank" on the Stockfish move-agreement metric, suggesting "reasoning over probability may have provided some strength" — [Gardner et al. 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v123/gardner20a/gardner20a.pdf).
- 2019 other winners' designs: LaSalle (2nd) "Maintains 32 probability distributions, one for the location of each piece" and senses the square with probability "closest to 50%" adjusted by heuristics ("potential check is more important than other squares"); Oracle (3rd) senses to "minimize the expected number of possible board states … with some heuristics to choose an alternative check-detecting sense if Oracle may be in check" and plays "the move that is recommended by Stockfish most across all the possible board states"; wbernar5 (5th) "Tracks up to 500 potential states … Senses to maximize the number of states eliminated in the worst case. Chooses the move with the best worst-Stockfish score" — [Gardner et al. 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v123/gardner20a/gardner20a.pdf).
- ROOKie (5th, 2022): "Each tracked board contributes votes of equal weight for up to 5 moves, against any moves that leave the king in check"; "Computing top moves is often much faster than analysis of position strength, enabling the bot to evaluate more boards" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).

**Belief-set sizes tracked / observed**
- 2019 organiser analysis capped tracking at 50,000 states; median possible opponent states: A Bot 12, Oracle 13, wbernar5 18, LaSalle 19, StrangeFish 19, Marmot 24, penumbra 48, genetic 49; "StrangeFish … had a median number of states 58% higher than A bot's and 46% higher than Oracle's but outperformed both of them" — [Gardner et al. 2020, Table 2](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v123/gardner20a/gardner20a.pdf).
- 2021 (cap 1M): "The top 11 performers kept this median below 100, typically at or below 20"; Fianchetto 9, StrangeFish2 15, penumbra 24, Kevin 13, Oracle 13, Gnash 18, wbernar5 5; trout 3,243; random 68,263 — [Perrotta et al. 2022, Table 3](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf).
- 2022: StrangeFish2 median 32 (rank 10 on this metric), Fianchetto 20, Kevin 16, Châteaux 21, ROOKie 29, Oracle 16; "while the top bots all appear to keep this number under 35, beyond that, the number-of-possible-board-states rank does not appear to be strongly correlated with tournament rank" — [Gardner et al. 2023, Table 3](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- Organisers' caveat: board-state count "is not the same as the number of game states or histories, which includes all of both players' senses"; "The number of possible game states inevitably grows exponentially each turn due to the private sense action"; "No bot has made much progress in representing or using it" — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf); [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).

**Information gathering — evidence**
- 2019: "Top algorithms generally incorporated sensing strategies that successfully minimized uncertainty … The top two approaches reduced this raw uncertainty metric less than some others"; "one's sensing strategy must go beyond minimizing the number of possible opponent states" — [Gardner et al. 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v123/gardner20a/gardner20a.pdf).
- 2021: "Several bots choose sense actions to minimize the expected number of remaining states, but most of the top performers instead choose sense actions with a more general game objective in mind"; "penumbra and StrangeFish2 used sense strategies with objectives other than minimization of possible board states, and exhibited overall tournament performance which exceeded their relative rank in median states" — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf).
- 2022: "simply minimizing uncertainty is insufficient. Accounting for the impact of exactly what one will do with an observation appears to be critical, in alignment with StrangeFish2's primary upgrade from previous years" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- Clark: "reducing uncertainty increases the odds of winning" (Figure 1 win rate vs state count); DSMCP "unifies all action selection" (sense and move chosen by the same search) while "Most RBC programs select sense actions and move actions in separate ways" — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810).

**King danger — evidence**
- Chess-engine bias: 2021 openings "e4 37% of the time, and of those games, white won 71%", d4 17% with "66% losses for white", c4 5% with "83% wins"; "As algorithms advance state evaluation functions specific to RBC rather than chess, it is likely that fewer games will open with d4, and more with c4" — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf).
- 2022: "Bots may benefit from being more aggressive and risk-taking than in standard chess (e.g., placing pieces where they would be less strategically located if seen but enable attacks if temporarily unseen). Another possibility is that bots benefit from moving more conservatively. For example, the optimal strategy in RBC may be to frequently protect against attacks that are not actually possible given the true board state" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- Engine-agreement vs rank: StrangeFish2 "only ranked 6 in chess-engine move agreement" (62%) yet won; Marmot (32%) and DynamicEntropy (29%) have "especially low move agreement among the top 10 bots", hypothesised to result from "explicitly keeping track of information sets in their search algorithms" — [Gardner et al. 2023, Table 3](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- The "strategy fusion" failure that per-board best-move aggregation produces: with the opponent knight on either d3 or f3 and the king attacked in both, capturing on either square scores ≈0 under 50/50 weighting while the king retreat (Kd2) scores ≈0.99, "which is completely separate from the best move for either perfect information state"; therefore "it is not sufficient to only evaluate the absolute best moves, but rather … all possible moves" (Section III-C, Figure 2) — [Bertram et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05864). Clark also warns that aggregating per-determinization inference "can easily lead to strategy fusion" (Section 5.1) — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810).
- StrangeFish2 ScoreConfig numbers above (into-check −7000 etc.), Oracle/LaSalle check-detecting senses, ROOKie's votes against moves leaving the king in check, Penumbra's forced-win static analysis — sources as cited above.

### Inferences
- (Inference) The recipe that has won every RBC competition is closer to the user's existing stack than to any deep-RL system: an exhaustive or capped hypothesis set, an engine evaluation per (board, move) pair, a risk-weighted aggregate (mean + worst-case), likelihood weights on boards derived from "how good is the opponent's position" (StrangeFish2: logistic of Stockfish score with 90th percentile at 3000 cp) or learned (Siamese, 53% top-1), and randomisation among near-equal moves. StrangeFish2's `BOARD_SET_LIMIT = 1000` with a backlog that is re-expanded only down to 30 boards is a concrete, proven answer to "what belief size to track on a time budget".
- (Inference) The learned-component evidence is consistent: learning helps as (i) an opponent model/likelihood weight over the hypothesis set (Kevin, JKU-CODA, Fianchetto's Lc0 prior), (ii) a policy prior inside search (Penumbra: −370 Elo when search is removed), and (iii) a cheap batch evaluator (Lc0 in Fianchetto) — but never as a replacement for search/engine evaluation (Bertram 2022 Elo 1330; uccchess 14th). For the user's recurrent policy, the highest-value role indicated by this literature is as the opponent-move model that sets particle weights, plus as a move-ordering prior for alpha-beta.
- (Inference) "King danger" in winners is handled by hard penalties and explicit threat detection, not by learned values; and the one documented structural failure (strategy fusion on king attacks) argues for evaluating every candidate move on every sampled board rather than taking per-board best moves — which is exactly what alpha-beta over a candidate list on each particle gives.
- (Inference) FoW differs from RBC in that information gathering is not a separate action; the RBC lesson "sense to maximise the expected outcome of the move you will then make" translates to scoring candidate moves by expected value after the vision update they induce (one-step lookahead over the observation partition), which StrangeFish2 computes in O(|moves| × |sample|) and which is affordable on 2 cores for samples of ~250 boards.
- (Inference) Hardware context: RBC bots ran on the authors' own machines (Fianchetto used a GPU for Lc0; Penumbra used GPU inference in batches of 256), so their absolute strengths are not directly transferable to a 2-core CPU budget; StrangeFish2 is the most CPU-friendly of the winners (Stockfish depth 7 per evaluation, 2–3 s min/40 s max per turn at 15-min clocks).

### Gaps
- No RBC report gives the per-bot hardware; parallelism and GPU use are only implied. Any 2-core feasibility claim about StrangeFish2 is extrapolation from its constants.
- Fianchetto has no public paper or code; its "POMDP belief update" is described only in one sentence.
- Kevin's 2022 network (ResNet+LSTM imitation of StrangeFish2 logs) has no published architecture, dataset size, or ablation; the competition table is the only evidence of its placing (3rd).
- 2023/2024 RBC competition reports were not surveyed (out of scope for the brief; rbc.jhuapl.edu/past_competitions lists later events).
- No source quantifies "king danger" as a separate metric; evidence is limited to the heuristics and the organisers' qualitative remarks above.

## Key Question 4 — IQL/CQL: what replay-only RL on game logs offers over behaviour cloning, and failure modes under distribution shift

### Takeaway
CQL diagnoses the core failure of naive off-policy RL on fixed logs — bootstrapping from out-of-distribution actions whose Q-values are erroneously optimistic — and fixes it with a penalty that makes the learned Q a lower bound; IQL avoids ever querying unseen actions by fitting an upper expectile of in-sample Q and extracting the policy by advantage-weighted regression. On D4RL both beat BC decisively only where the data are mixed-quality and require stitching (hopper-medium-replay: BC 18.1 vs IQL 94.7/CQL 95.0; AntMaze large: BC 0 vs IQL 40–48), and they roughly tie BC/10%BC on expert-quality data. For game logs from heterogeneous bots the stitching benefit is real, but both methods silently depend on dataset coverage and on the state being Markov, which an infostate/belief summary in FoW chess is not.

### Cited Findings
- CQL motivation: standard off-policy methods fail on static datasets "due to bootstrapping from out-of-distribution actions" producing "erroneously optimistic value function estimates"; the policy "exploits out-of-distribution (OOD) actions with erroneously inflated Q-values" (Section 1, 3) — [CQL, arXiv 2006.04779](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.04779v3).
- CQL objective: minimise Q under a distribution μ(a|s) and maximise it under the behaviour distribution π̂_β(a|s), plus the Bellman error; practical CQL(H) adds "α·E_s[log Σ_a exp(Q(s,a)) − E_{a∼π̂_β}[Q(s,a)]]"; "less than 20 lines of code" on top of SAC/DQN; Theorem 3.2: expected Q under the learned policy lower-bounds its true value when μ = π; the backups are "gap-expanding" — [CQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.04779v3).
- CQL empirical claims: "2–5 times higher final return" on medium-expert tasks; on Adroit it was "the only method outperforming behavioral cloning"; Atari with 1% data "36×" prior; the paper also states that "Behavioral Cloning achieves better performance on expert datasets" — [CQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.04779v3).
- CQL tuning: a Lagrange variant adapts α so that "if the expected difference in Q-values is less than the specified threshold τ, α will adjust to be close to 0"; τ = 10.0 for gym, 5.0 for Kitchen/Adroit, fixed α = 1.0–4.0 for Atari; acknowledged open issues include theoretical analysis with neural networks and "potential for overfitting in long-horizon offline settings" — [CQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.04779v3).
- IQL method: value loss L_V(ψ) = E[L₂^τ(Q(s,a) − V(s))] with asymmetric (expectile) loss, τ > 0.5 upweights higher-value actions (Eq. 5); Q loss L_Q(θ) = E[(r + γV(s′) − Q(s,a))²] "only uses dataset actions, never samples new ones" (Eq. 6); policy by advantage-weighted BC, L_π(φ) = E[exp(β(Q − V)) log π_φ(a|s)] (Eq. 7); Theorem 3: lim_{τ→1} V_τ(s) = max_{a: π_β(a|s)>0} Q*(s,a), i.e. the optimum restricted to dataset-supported actions (Section 4) — [IQL, arXiv 2110.06169](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.06169).
- IQL hyperparameters: τ = 0.7 (locomotion), 0.9 (AntMaze); β = 3.0 (locomotion), 10.0 (AntMaze); 2-layer 256-unit MLPs; lr 3e-4 with cosine schedule for the actor; soft target updates 0.005; "~20 minutes for 1M updates on single GPU (4× faster than CQL reimplementation)" — [IQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.06169).
- IQL Table 1 (D4RL normalised score; BC / 10%BC / CQL / IQL): hopper-medium 52.9 / 56.9 / 58.5 / 66.3; walker2d-medium 75.3 / 75.0 / 72.5 / 78.3; halfcheetah-medium-replay 36.6 / 40.6 / 45.5 / 44.2; hopper-medium-replay 18.1 / 75.9 / 95.0 / 94.7; walker2d-medium-replay 26.0 / 62.5 / 77.2 / 73.9; hopper-medium-expert 52.5 / 110.9 / 105.4 / 91.5; walker2d-medium-expert 107.5 / 109.0 / 108.8 / 109.6; locomotion total 466.7 / 666.2 / 698.5 / 692.4; antmaze-medium-play 0.0 / 5.4 / 61.2 / 71.2; antmaze-large-play 0.0 / 0.0 / 15.8 / 39.6; antmaze-large-diverse 0.0 / 6.0 / 14.9 / 47.5; AntMaze total 100.2 / 134.2 / 303.6 / 378.0; Kitchen total BC 154.5 / CQL 144.6 / IQL 159.8; Adroit total BC 104.5 / CQL 93.6 / IQL 118.1 — [IQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.06169).
- Why stitching matters: some domains "contain very few or no near-optimal trajectories" and "require 'stitching' parts of suboptimal trajectories that travel between different states to find a path"; one-step methods work only with "a significant fraction of near-optimal trajectories in the dataset"; Figure 3: "estimating a larger expectile τ is crucial for antmaze tasks that require dynamical programming ('stitching')" — [IQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.06169).
- Game-domain evidence on learning from logs without search: Bertram et al. 2022 (supervised on public RBC logs + PPO self-play, no search) reached Elo 1330, rank 27 — [arXiv 2208.02029](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02029); Penumbra's ablation shows the policy-only agent at 1376 vs 1747 with search — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810); Clark frames opponent-specific play as "related offline reinforcement learning (RL) problems" and warns "Modeling an opponent poorly could be catastrophic" — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810). Kevin (3rd, 2022) used pure imitation on StrangeFish2 logs for the opponent model and Stockfish EV for its own move — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- The 2022 organisers on offline/no-search methods generally (NFSP, Deep-CFR, DREAM, ESCHER, R-NaD): they "have not achieved superhuman play in complex games without search, and it is not clear that these approaches are practical on large games like RBC without extraordinary hardware" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).

### Inferences
- (Inference) For FoW chess logs, the relevant D4RL analogue is "medium-replay": mixed-strength players, few near-optimal games. There BC collapses (18–37) while IQL/CQL recover 74–95, so a value head trained with IQL-style in-sample backups should beat plain BC *as a value estimator* for the particle-set leaf evaluation. Expected gain on policy quality is smaller unless the logs contain exploitable stitching (e.g., good openings from one bot, good endgames from another).
- (Inference) The known failure modes map directly: (a) value extrapolation — any Q(s,a) for moves never played in a given belief context is unreliable; IQL's expectile avoids querying them, CQL penalises them; (b) non-Markov state — the "state" in logs is an observation history; using a belief summary as the state makes the Bellman target depend on the opponent's hidden policy, so values learned from logs are opponent-population-conditional and will be mis-calibrated against a new opponent (Clark's "catastrophic" warning); (c) coverage — τ→1 recovers the optimum only over dataset-supported actions (IQL Theorem 3), so aggressive king-exposing lines absent from logs are invisible to the learner; (d) over-conservatism — CQL's α/τ thresholds are domain-tuned and the paper flags long-horizon overfitting.
- (Inference) Inference cost is not the issue: IQL/CQL produce an ordinary policy/value net; on 2 cores the cost is the same as the user's current recurrent policy. The decision is about training signal quality, and the literature says: use RL-from-logs for value/opponent-likelihood heads, keep search for the action.

### Gaps
- No published work applies IQL or CQL to RBC/FoW chess or to any chess-variant game log; the D4RL evidence is the only quantitative basis.
- The CQL paper's full Table 1 numbers (BC vs CQL per task) were not returned by the extraction tool; the IQL table (which includes BC, 10%BC, CQL and IQL columns) is used instead.
- Neither paper analyses the non-Markov/partial-observability case; the inference about belief-state non-stationarity is the author's.

## Key Question 5 — Cross-cutting: what is feasible under 2 cores / 4 GiB / 15 s init / 180 + 2 clocks

### Takeaway
Of the surveyed systems, Obscuro (3 threads on a desktop i5, 3+2, no GPU, no training) and StrangeFish2 (Stockfish depth 7 per evaluation, ≤1000 tracked boards with a backlog, 250-board scoring sample, 3–40 s per turn at 15-minute clocks) are the only ones whose published compute profiles are within reach of the target; ReBeL, Penumbra/Châteaux (GPU inference batches of 256, 4,000 nodes/s on GPU hardware) and Fianchetto (Lc0 on GPU) are not.

### Cited Findings
- Obscuro: single desktop 6-core i5, 1 CFR thread + 2 expansion threads, 3+2 clocks; strength curve −50 Elo at 1/16 s/move to +2 Elo at 16 s/move relative to 5 s/move; avg |P| 17,264 and 1.07M-node / 14k-infoset trees at 5 s/move; no learning phase — [Obscuro](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2).
- StrangeFish2: `search_depth 7` Stockfish ply per (board, move); `BOARD_SET_LIMIT 1000`; `SCORE_SAMPLE_LIMIT 250`; `SENSE_SAMPLE_LIMIT 2500`; per-turn time min 3 s / max 40 s dividing remaining time over 16 turns; required to support 4 simultaneous games under 15-minute clocks — [StrangeFish2 source](https://github.com/ginoperrotta/reconchess-strangefish2); [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).
- Penumbra: "Inference is done in batches of 256 … The program processes approximately 4,000 nodes per second"; training and evaluation on four RTX 2080 Ti; random play beyond 9M possible states — [Clark 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810).
- ReBeL: 90 DGX-1 × 8 V100 for HUNL data generation; belief input 2×1326 per player; "intractable in games such as Recon Chess" — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544).
- Fianchetto: Lc0 evaluations "with the additional option of evaluating multiple boards in parallel on a GPU" — [Perrotta et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a/perrotta22a.pdf).
- RBC organisers on search in RBC-like games: "online search has been shown to greatly improve play strength (Silver et al., 2017), these methods [offline equilibrium learners] have not achieved superhuman play in complex games without search" — [Gardner et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a/gardner23a.pdf).

### Inferences
- (Inference) A 2-core deployment that keeps the existing particle filter + alpha-beta and adds (i) StrangeFish2-style risk-weighted aggregation with likelihood weights, (ii) a one-step "expected outcome after the induced observation" term for move choice, (iii) random selection among near-equal moves, and (iv) optionally an Obscuro-style sampled-infoset CFR layer with a retained tree, follows the two empirically strongest CPU-only designs. The 15 s init is enough to load a pre-computed opening/score cache (StrangeFish2 ships `load_score_cache` / `load_opening_book`) but not to train anything.
- (Inference) The 4 GiB limit argues against Obscuro-scale trees (1M nodes) unless node storage is compact; a StrangeFish2-scale cache (≤1000 boards, 250 scored) is trivially within budget.
- (Inference) The neural recurrent policy is best used where the RBC evidence shows learned models paying off on CPU: as an opponent-move likelihood for particle reweighting (Kevin, JKU-CODA) and as a move-ordering/prior for alpha-beta and candidate selection (Penumbra's m-mixing), not as the final action selector.

### Gaps
- No source reports a direct 2-core or memory-limited benchmark of any of these systems; all feasibility statements above are extrapolations from published constants and scaling curves.
