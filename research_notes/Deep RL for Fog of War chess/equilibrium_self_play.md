## Key question 1: Why does naive self-play policy gradient fail in imperfect-information zero-sum games, and what mechanism in R-NaD / MMD fixes it? What are the exact loss terms and the reference-policy schedule?

### Takeaway
Unregularised learning dynamics (replicator / FoReL / gradient descent-ascent) in zero-sum games are Poincaré-recurrent: they orbit the equilibrium rather than converge, and this is a property of the dynamics, not of function approximation. Both R-NaD and MMD fix this by adding a KL-type regulariser toward a reference ("regularisation"/"magnet") policy, which makes the regularised game strongly monotone so the last iterate converges exponentially to a *regularised* equilibrium; Nash is then recovered by repeatedly moving the reference policy to the latest fixed point (R-NaD), by annealing the temperature, or by letting the magnet trail the iterate (MMD).

### Cited Findings

**The failure mode (cycling / recurrence)**
- DeepNash abstract: R-NaD "converges to an approximate Nash equilibrium, instead of 'cycling' around it, by directly modifying the underlying multi-agent learning dynamics." — [DeepNash, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- DeepNash Sec. 2.1: combining model-free RL and self-play "has been empirically challenging to stabilize ... when scaling up to complex games, as for example Capture the flag, Dota and StarCraft". Prior work stabilised it "either by training against past versions of the agent ..., or by adding reward-shaping ... or expert data"; "such approaches lack theoretical foundations, remain hard to tune and do not easily generalize". Minimising exploitability directly "requires estimating an agent's best response during training, which is computationally intractable in Stratego." Instead one can "define a learning update rule that induces a dynamical system for which there exists a so-called Lyapunov function." — [DeepNash, Sec. 2.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- The theory paper underlying R-NaD: "we investigate the Follow the Regularized Leader dynamics in sequential imperfect information games (IIG). We generalize existing results of Poincaré recurrence from normal-form games to zero-sum two-player imperfect information games", and show "how adapting the reward (by adding a regularization term) of the game can give strong convergence guarantees in monotone games" and "how this reward adaptation technique can be leveraged to build algorithms that converge exactly to the Nash equilibrium." — [Perolat et al., ICML 2021, abstract](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/perolat21a.html)
- MMD, remark after Theorem 3.4: "Note α > 0 is necessary to converge to the solution. If α = 0 ... Algorithm 3.1 with ψ(z) = ½‖z‖² becomes projected gradient descent ascent, which is known to diverge or cycle for any positive stepsize." — [MMD, Sec. 3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- MMD, Appendix J (deep experiments): "PPO with RLlib's default hyperparameters does not appear to decrease exploitability over time. This is not necessarily surprising, as RL algorithms do not generally converge in two-player zero-sum games." After 10M steps PPO's approximate exploitability was 100±0 (×10⁻²) in 3x3 Abrupt Dark Hex and 90±4 in Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe, i.e. no better than a deterministic first-legal-action bot (100) and worse than uniform random (74 / 78). — [MMD, Appendix J, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- NFSP Sec. 4.2: DQN in Leduc "learns a deterministic, greedy strategy ... imperfect-information games generally require stochastic strategies"; "DQN's deterministic strategy is highly exploitable ... DQN's average behaviour does not approach a Nash equilibrium either." Cause given: DQN agents "exclusively generate self-play experience according to their ε-greedy strategies. These experiences are both highly correlated over time, and highly focused on a narrow distribution of states." Also: "TD-Gammon ... is sound in two-player zero-sum perfect-information games, it does not generally converge in games with imperfect information." — [NFSP, Secs. 4.2, 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01121)
- NFSP Appendix A: full-width "XFP with stepsize 1 is equivalent to a full-width iterated best response algorithm. While this algorithm converges to a Nash equilibrium in finite perfect-information two-player zero-sum games, the results suggest that with imperfect information this is not generally the case." — [NFSP, Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01121)
- AlphaStar: "Self-play algorithms, similar to those used in chess and Go, learn rapidly but may chase cycles (for example, where A defeats B, and B defeats C, but A loses to C) indefinitely without making progress." — [AlphaStar, main text "Learning Algorithm"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- PSRO Sec. 1: independent RL "local environments are non-stationary and non-Markovian resulting in a loss of convergence guarantees for many algorithms, and the policies found can overfit to the other agents' policies". — [PSRO, Sec. 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)

**R-NaD mechanism (normal-form statement, DeepNash Sec. 2.2 and "Details on R-NaD in Two-Player Zero-Sum Normal Form Games")**
- Step 1, reward transformation with regularisation policy π_reg and parameter η > 0:
  r^i(π^i, π^{−i}, a^i, a^{−i}) = r^i(a^i, a^{−i}) − η log(π^i(a^i)/π^i_reg(a^i)) + η log(π^{−i}(a^{−i})/π^{−i}_reg(a^{−i})). "Note that this transformed reward is policy-dependent." The initial π_reg "can be chosen arbitrary (as long as all actions have a non-zero probability)." — [DeepNash, Sec. 2.2 and Methods p. 24–25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Step 2, dynamics: replicator dynamics on the modified game, dπ^i_τ(a^i)/dτ = π^i_τ(a^i)[Q^i_{π_τ}(a^i) − Σ_b π^i_τ(b)Q^i_{π_τ}(b)], "equivalent to RL algorithms ... also known as Follow the Regularized Leader". "Thanks to the reward transformation this system has a unique fixed point π_fix and convergence to it is guaranteed," proven by the Lyapunov function H_{π_fix}(π) = Σ_i Σ_a π^i_fix(a) log(π^i_fix(a)/π^i(a)), with dH/dτ ≤ −η H, "which means that the distance to the fixed point will decrease exponentially to 0." "However, this fixed point is not yet a Nash equilibrium of the original game." — [DeepNash, Sec. 2.2, Methods p. 25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Step 3, update: π_{n+1,reg} = π_{n,fix}. "This process generates a sequence of fixed points n → π_{n,fix}, which is known to converge to the Nash equilibrium ... as the sequence of distances Σ_i KL(π^i_nash, π^i_{n,fix}) can be proven (34) to decrease to 0." — [DeepNash, Methods p. 25–26](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Role of η: "The regularization parameter η is fixed throughout this process. ... a higher value gives more stable and faster convergence to the fixed point. On the other hand a higher η results in a fixed point that is more biased towards the regularization policy, which means one might need more overall iterations to approach the Nash equilibrium sufficiently close." — [DeepNash, Methods p. 26](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Worked matching-pennies example: starting from π_{0,reg}[H,T] = [0.999, 0.001] with η = 0.2, the first fixed point is π⁰_{0,fix} = [0.896, 0.104], π¹_{0,fix} = [0.263, 0.737]; the log-Lyapunov function decreases during learning (Fig. 2c). — [DeepNash, Sec. 2.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)

**R-NaD at scale: the exact extensive-form loss terms (DeepNash "Model-free Reinforcement Learning with Regularized Nash Dynamics", pp. 27–30)**
- Per-timestep reward transform: for acting player i = ψ_t: r_t^i − η log(π(a|o_t)/π_{m,reg}(a|o_t)); for the non-acting player: r_t^i + η log(π(a|o_t)/π_{m,reg}(a|o_t)). Compactly r^i_{π_m,reg}(π,h,a) = r^i(h,a) + (1 − 2·1_{i=ψ(h)}) η log(π(a|o(h))/π_{m,reg}(a|o(h))), starting with π_{−1,reg} = π_{0,reg}. — [DeepNash, Sec. 2.3.2, p. 28](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Smooth reference switch: within iteration m of length Δ_m, at step n ∈ [0, Δ_m] the reward is r^i_{reg,n} = α_n r^i_{π_m,reg} + (1 − α_n) r^i_{π_{m−1},reg} with α_n = min(1, 2n/Δ_m) "in order to smooth the transition between a regularization policy to another" (equivalently π_{m,n,reg} = α_n π_{m,reg} + (1 − α_n) π_{m−1,reg}). — [DeepNash, p. 28](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Critic / Q estimator: a two-player adaptation of V-trace (Eqs. 1–5), run backward over the *full trajectory* "without any bootstrapping. This ensures that these estimates have a minimum bias." The Q-estimate used for the policy update is
  Q̂^i_t(a) = −η log(π_{θ_n}(a|o_t)/π_{m,n,reg}(a|o_t)) + [1_{a=a_t}/μ_t(a_t)] ( r^i_t + η log(π_{θ_n}(a|o_t)/π_{m,n,reg}(a|o_t)) + (π(a_t|o_t)/μ_t(a_t))(r̂^i_{t+1} + v̂^i_{t+1}) − v(o_t) ) + v(o_t)   (Eq. 5),
  with clipped ratios ρ_t = min(ρ̄, (π/μ)ξ_{t+1}), c_t = min(c̄, (π/μ)ξ_{t+1}) (Eq. 4). Estimates are computed from the *target* network: v̂, Q̂ = Υ(T_n, π_{θ_n,target}, v_{θ_n,target}). — [DeepNash, pp. 29–30](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Value loss: l_critic(θ) = Σ_{i=1}^2 (1/t_effective) Σ_t 1_{i=ψ_t} ‖v_θ(o_t) − v̂^i_t‖. — [DeepNash, p. 30](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Policy update: Neural Replicator Dynamics (NeuRD) on the logits l_θ, with update direction Λ_n = −lr ∇l_critic(θ_n) + Σ_i (1/t_eff) Σ_t Σ_a ∇̂θ( l_{θ_n}(a,o_t), Clip(Q̂_{t,n}(a,o_t), c_clipNeuRD), lr, β ), where ∇̂θ(z(θ), η, β) = η ∇_θ z(θ) · 1_{z(θ + η∇z) ∈ [−β, β]} (i.e. the logit is pushed by the advantage only while it stays inside a band of half-width β) and Clip(·, c) = min(max(·, −c), c). Then θ_{n+1} = Adam(θ_n, Clip(Λ_n, c_clip gradient), b1, b2, ε) and θ_{n+1,target} = γ_averaging θ_{n+1} + (1 − γ_averaging) θ_{n,target}. — [DeepNash, p. 30](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Reference-policy schedule: "After the dynamics steps ... we define the new fixed point policy as π_{m,fix} = π_{θ_{n=Δ_m},target}. The next regularisation policy is defined as π_{m+1,reg} = π_{m,fix} and we go on to the next step (m+1) starting from the parameters θ_0, θ_{0,target} and the state of the optimizer being the ones we finished the iteration m with." — [DeepNash, p. 30](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- DeepNash hyperparameters (Table 2): η = 0.2; Δ_m = 10k learner steps for m ≤ 100, 100k for 100 < m ≤ 165, 35k for m > 165; max 7.21M learner steps; lr = 0.00005; c_clip gradient = 10000; NeuRD β = 2.0, c_clipNeuRD = 10000; Adam b1 = 0.0, b2 = 0.999, ε = 1e−8; target network γ_averaging = 0.001; V-trace ρ̄ = c̄ = 1.0; t_max = 3600; batch = 768 trajectories per step; fine-tuning t_res = 0.03, n_disc = 32. — [DeepNash, Table 2, p. 32](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Positioning vs. other families (DeepNash Related Work): NeuRD alone "is proven to converge to a Nash equilibrium in the time average"; "this work uses NeuRD combined with the regularization idea laid out in (34) to converge in the last iterate." Regret methods (Deep CFR, DREAM, ARMAC) "rely on importance sampling terms ... this importance weight might become very large in games with a long horizon such as Stratego" and need an average strategy that is "memory heavy ... or error prone". — [DeepNash, Related Work p. 22–23](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)

**OpenSpiel R-NaD implementation (confirms the schedule and loss in code)**
- Default config: `trajectory_max = 10`, `policy_network_layers = (256, 256)`, `batch_size = 256`, `learning_rate = 0.00005`, Adam `b1 = 0.0, b2 = 0.999, eps = 10e-8`, `clip_gradient = 10_000`, `target_network_avg = 0.001`, `entropy_schedule_repeats = (1,)`, `entropy_schedule_size = (20_000,)`, `eta_reward_transform = 0.2`, `NerdConfig(beta = 2.0, clip = 10_000)`, `c_vtrace = 1.0`, `FineTuning(from_learner_steps = -1 [disabled], policy_threshold = 0.03, ...)`. — [OpenSpiel rnad.py v1.5, `RNaDConfig`, `NerdConfig`, `AdamConfig`, `FineTuning`](https://github.com/google-deepmind/open_spiel/blob/v1.5/open_spiel/python/algorithms/rnad/rnad.py)
- The reference policy is two frozen parameter sets `params_prev` / `params_prev_`; the regularisation log-ratio is `log_policy_reg = log_pi - (alpha * log_pi_prev + (1 - alpha) * log_pi_prev_)` with code comment "For the stability reasons, reward changes smoothly between iterations. The mixing between old and new reward transform is a convex combination parametrised by alpha." — [rnad.py `loss()`](https://github.com/google-deepmind/open_spiel/blob/v1.5/open_spiel/python/algorithms/rnad/rnad.py)
- `EntropySchedule.__call__` returns `alpha = min(2*(learner_step - iteration_start)/iteration_size, 1.0)` and `update_target_net` true on the last step of each iteration; on that step `params_prev, params_prev_ = (params_target, params_prev)` — i.e. the *target (EMA) network* becomes the new reference and the old reference becomes the "previous reference" used in the interpolation. The target net is updated by `optax.sgd(target_network_avg)` on `params_target - params`. — [rnad.py `EntropySchedule`, `update_parameters`](https://github.com/google-deepmind/open_spiel/blob/v1.5/open_spiel/python/algorithms/rnad/rnad.py)
- Inside `v_trace`, the regulariser enters as `eta_reg_entropy = -eta * sum(pi * log_policy_reg) * player_others` added to the reward (`reward_uncorrected = reward + gamma*carry.reward_uncorrected + eta_reg_entropy`) and as a per-action term `eta_log_policy = -eta * log_policy_reg * player_others` added to the learning output used as the Q-vector for NeuRD. The code calls v-trace with `lambda_=1.0, c=c_vtrace (1.0), rho=np.inf`. — [rnad.py `v_trace`, `loss`](https://github.com/google-deepmind/open_spiel/blob/v1.5/open_spiel/python/algorithms/rnad/rnad.py)
- NeuRD loss in code: `adv_pi = q_vr - sum(pi * q_vr)`; clipped to ±`clip`; stop-gradient; logits centred by the mean of *legal* logits; `apply_force_with_threshold` lets a logit be increased only if `logit - mean < threshold` and decreased only if `logit - mean > -threshold` (threshold = β = 2.0); loss = −Σ_a legal·logit·stop_grad(clipped_force), normalised by number of valid acting steps. — [rnad.py `get_loss_nerd`, `apply_force_with_threshold`](https://github.com/google-deepmind/open_spiel/blob/v1.5/open_spiel/python/algorithms/rnad/rnad.py)

**MMD mechanism (Sokota et al.)**
- Update rule in RL language (Eq. 10): π_{t+1} = argmax_π E_{A∼π} q_t(A) − α KL(π, ρ) − (1/η) KL(π, π_t), "where π_t is the current policy, q_t is the Q-value vector for time t, and ρ is a magnet policy." Parameter-space form (Eq. 11): θ_{t+1} = argmin_θ ⟨∇L(θ_t), θ⟩ + (α/2)‖θ − ζ‖² + (1/2η)‖θ − θ_t‖². Closed forms (Eq. 12): π_{t+1} ∝ [π_t ρ^{αη} e^{ηq_t}]^{1/(1+αη)}, θ_{t+1} = [θ_t + αηζ − η∇L(θ_t)]/(1 + αη). "The stepsize η dictates the extent to which moving away from the current iterate is penalized; the regularization temperature α dictates the extent to which being far away from the magnet ... is penalized". — [MMD, Sec. 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Theorem 3.4 (linear last-iterate convergence): under monotone, L-smooth F and 1-relatively-strongly-convex g, if η ≤ α/L² then B_ψ(z*; z_{t+1}) ≤ (1/(1+ηα))^t B_ψ(z*; z_1). Corollary D.6 generalises to arbitrary strong-convexity constants. — [MMD, Sec. 3.1, App. D.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Proposition 2.4: finding a (reduced normal-form) QRE of a two-player zero-sum EFG is a strongly-monotone VI over the sequence form with dilated entropy; F is max_ij|A_ij|-smooth. Hence sequence-form MMD with dilated entropy "converges to the α-QRE exponentially fast"; this is "the first linear convergence results to quantal response equilibria (QREs) in EFGs for a first order method." — [MMD, Secs. 1, 2.4, 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Behavioural-form (per-information-state) MMD: "If ρ is uniform, a fixed point of this instantiation is an α-AQRE"; convergence observed empirically "(despite a lack of guarantees)" with η = α/10 (Fig. 1 bottom row; App. G.4: "convergence is not monotonic, it is roughly linear over large time scales"). — [MMD, Sec. 3.3, Sec. 4, App. G.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Recovering Nash: (i) annealed temperature and stepsize with constant magnet, or (ii) constant α, η with a moving magnet. Both "exhibit last-iterate convergent behavior" and "perform competitively with (or better than) CFR" (CFR+ still best). — [MMD, Sec. 4 "Exploitability Experiments"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Moving magnet details (App. H.5): π_{t+1}(h) ∝ [π_t(h) ρ_t(h)^{ηα} e^{ηq}]^{1/(1+ηα)} with ρ_{t+1}(h) ∝ ρ_t(h)^{1−η̃} π_{t+1}(h)^{η̃}; used α = 1, η = 0.1, η̃ = 0.05; "moving the magnet behind the current iterate also appears to induce convergence. Furthermore, convergence may occur at a much faster rate than that which is induced by annealing the temperature." — [MMD, App. H.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Annealing schedules used (tabular): Diplomacy full feedback η = 1/10, α_t = 1/(5√t); Kuhn η_t = α_t = 1/√t; Leduc η_t = 1/√t, α_t = 5/√t (unweighted); black-box EFG: Leduc η_t = 1/√t, α_t = 300/√t. — [MMD, App. H.1, H.3, H.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Deep MMD = PPO with one change: "We implemented MMD as a modification of RLlib's PPO implementation by changing the adaptive forward KL regularization to a reverse KL regularization" (Sec. 4); App. J: "changing the adaptive forward KL regularization to a constant reverse KL regularization and setting η_t, α_t according the following schedule η_t = 0.05·sqrt(10 million / t), α_t = 0.05·sqrt(10 million / t), where t is the number of time steps—not the number of episodes. Otherwise, we used the default hyperparameters." — [MMD, Sec. 4, App. J](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Appendix L gives the loss explicitly. KL-PPO: E_t[(π(a_t|s_t)/π_old(a_t|s_t)) Â_t + αH(π(s_t)) − β KL(π_old(s_t), π(s_t))]. MMD (negative-entropy mirror map, uniform magnet): E_t[(π(a_t|s_t)/π_old(a_t|s_t)) Â_t + αH(π(s_t)) − β KL(π(s_t), π_old(s_t))], "where β acts as an inverse stepsize." MDPO is the same without the entropy term; "in the case that it does include such an entropy term, MDPO and MMD coincide." — [MMD, App. L](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Single-agent sanity check: MMD-as-PPO with reverse-KL coefficient 1/η = 0.001 (Atari, α = 0.01) and 1/η = 0.1 (Mujoco, α = 0.0001) performed comparably to PPO (Table 1). — [MMD, App. I](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Sensitivity to α and estimator: "all three algorithms converge linearly with faster rates for larger values of alpha" (G.1) but larger α means a more regularised (biased) QRE; with sampled (black-box) feedback, "biased Q-value estimates generally perform best, followed by an unbiased estimate with baseline, followed by an unbiased estimate without baseline" (H.2); on-policy Monte-Carlo MMD "causes unbounded gradients" (H.2). — [MMD, App. G.1, G.2, H.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Relation to R-NaD / F-FoReL (Perolat 2021): "MMD offers the advantage that it is decentralizable, whereas the version of F-FoReL that Pérolat et al. (2021) present is not. In terms of scalability, MMD ... only requires approximating bounded quantities; in contrast, F-FoReL requires estimating an arbitrarily accumulating sum." Convergence theory: "we prove discrete-time linear convergence for NFGs, while Pérolat et al. (2021) give continuous-time linear convergence for EFGs using counterfactual values; neither possesses the desired discrete-time result for EFGs using action values." Empirically "F-FoReL's best result in Leduc is an exploitability of about 0.08 after 200,000 iterations—it takes MMD fewer than 1,000 iterations to achieve the same value." — [MMD, Sec. 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)

### Inferences
- (Inference) R-NaD and MMD are the same idea in different coordinates: both add −η·log(π/π_ref) (R-NaD, inside the reward) or −α·KL(π‖ρ) (MMD, inside the objective) and both recover Nash by moving the reference toward the current iterate. R-NaD's "update step every Δ_m steps with linear blend" and MMD's "moving magnet ρ ← ρ^{1−η̃}π^{η̃}" are a discrete-jump vs. continuous-EMA version of the same reference update. MMD's own Sec. 5 comparison supports treating them as siblings.
- (Inference) The reason naive PG/self-play "works" in Go/chess but not here is that perfect-information zero-sum games have pure-strategy minimax solutions reachable by iterated best response, whereas imperfect-information games require mixed strategies, and iterated best response / replicator flows orbit mixed equilibria (NFSP App. A, MMD Thm 3.4 remark, Perolat 2021 abstract taken together).
- (Inference) The "exact loss change" relative to PPO is small on paper (one KL term with a reversed direction, plus a frozen reference and a schedule), but DeepNash additionally changes three things that are *not* required by the theory and were engineering choices for Stratego: NeuRD logit-space updates with the β-band, full-episode unbootstrapped V-trace, and a target network as the reference. The OpenSpiel defaults show the smaller "research" configuration (batch 256, 20k steps/iteration, 2×256 MLP).

### Gaps
- Neither paper gives a principled rule for choosing η (R-NaD) or α (MMD) for a new game; DeepNash used η = 0.2 and the MMD deep runs used 0.05·sqrt(10M/t) with no sweep reported. The MMD authors list "an adaptive mechanism for adapting the stepsize, temperature, and magnet" as future work (App. M).
- No ablation in DeepNash of Δ_m (iteration length) or of the α_n blend; the three-phase Δ_m schedule (10k/100k/35k) is reported without justification.
- No discrete-time convergence proof exists for behavioural-form (per-infostate, action-value) MMD or for R-NaD with function approximation; both are empirical at scale.

## Key question 2: How did DeepNash handle partial observability (what the actor/critic condition on; was the critic privileged), and what was its training-infrastructure scale?

### Takeaway
DeepNash is *not* privileged: policy and value share one convolutional U-Net torso fed only the acting player's observation (own pieces, policy-independent public-information tensors for both sides, last 40 moves, a few scalars); there is no belief model and no opponent-state input to the critic. Partial observability was handled by hand-built, policy-independent "public information" channels plus a 40-move history window, and known memory failures were patched at test time with heuristics. Training used 768 TPU learner nodes + 256 TPU actor nodes, 7.21M learner steps at batch 768 full games (up to 3600 steps each), with actors in C++.

### Cited Findings
- Game size and reward: Stratego has "10^535 possible states"; "1066 possible deployments"; the agent is trained with "non-standard rules: a game automatically ends in a draw either after 2000 total moves, or 200 consecutive moves have been made without any piece being attacked." The trajectory contains per-step rewards (r_t^1, r_t^2) only; "the trajectories ... are the only data the agent will leverage". — [DeepNash, Sec. 1, 2.3.1, "Drawing rules" p. 34](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Observation (Table 3), stacked into a 10×10×82 tensor: lakes (10×10); own private piece tensor Prv_i (10×10×12); opponent public information Pub_{−i} (10×10×12); own public information Pub_i "this informs i on the information −i has on i's pieces" (10×10×12); last 40 moves (10×10×40); scalars broadcast to planes: game-length ratio, moves-since-last-attack ratio, phase, select-vs-target indicator; previously selected piece (10×10). — [DeepNash, Table 3 p. 37](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Public information is *policy independent*: Pub_i[r,c,t] "is the probability that square (r,c) holds a piece of player i of type t, under uniform sampling of piecetype assignments consistent with the move sequence of the game so far. The requirement here is just that the move sequence would be legal, not that it would be rational or in accordance with some policy." Computed in closed form (Eq. 6) from counts of unrevealed moved/unmoved pieces. "Note that these deductions are policy independent, and exclude heuristics which depend on the opponent's policy". — [DeepNash, "Neural Network Input Representation" pp. 35–36](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Model-free, no belief model: "The model-free part implies that we don't build an explicit opponent model tracking belief space (calculating a likelihood of the opponent's state)". — [DeepNash, Sec. 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Shared torso and non-privileged value head: "A torso module first processes the observation to produce a board game embedding ... 10 × 10 and channel dimension 256. This representation is then provided to three policy head modules specialized by game phase and a value-head module." "The value head is used during training ... takes as input the concatenation of the board game embedding, the no-attack ratio, and the one-hot representation of the selected piece." "we used a single value head for the three policies to reduce the agent's memory footprint." — [DeepNash, "Network Modules" pp. 38–39](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Architecture: U-Net/pyramid of residual conv/deconv blocks (torso: N = 2 outer blocks, M = 2 inner blocks, C = 256/320); policy heads N = 1, M = 0, final 3×3 conv to 1 channel, illegal logits set to −∞, softmax; value head N = 0, M = 0, flattened then linear to a scalar. Action space is 100 squares; play-phase moves decomposed into select-piece then select-destination; observations are player-centric (blue rotated 180°) so "a single agent ... can play both sides". — [DeepNash, "Agent action space", "Player-centric observations", "Neural Implementation" pp. 34–41](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Off-policy / replay: "The IMPALA architecture (44) is adapted to the needs of the R-NaD algorithm, namely storing full episodes in the replay buffer. The learner reads a batch of full episodes, splits them into the sequence of ordered chunks of fixed length (mini batches), and computes the parameter updates on that sequence in a reverse order." Variable-length games are padded; a forward pass "to recompute the exact network (LSTM) state for each timestep and trajectory statistics" (outside gradient computation) precedes a backward pass computing gradients per chunk; gradients accumulated and applied once per batch. — [DeepNash, "Infrastructure for learning" p. 31, "Full games learning" pp. 43–44](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Infrastructure: Sebulba Podracer architecture with Actors, Replay Buffer, Learner, Evaluators; "Actors run environment and inference and use CPU and a small amount of accelerator FLOPs"; JAX `pmap` SIMD with gradient averaging across learner machines; "To train the final agent we used 768 TPU nodes used for Learners and 256 TPU nodes for Actors." "The Actors environment-agent loop is implemented in C++ using fibers and the native open spiel (80) interfaces." — [DeepNash, "Infrastructure and Setup" pp. 41–43](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Learner steps / batch: 7.21M learner steps, 768 trajectories per step, t_max = 3600 (Table 2). — [DeepNash, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Memory limitation acknowledged: "in some cases the observation presented to the neural network ... does not contain sufficient history to allow a detailed assessment of a Stratego state. This is an area we think has considerable potential for further performance improvement, by using agent architectures that can better cope with long memory." Fix: a test-time "memory heuristic" (e.g. a Spy would attack a known Marshal; a non-attack implies the piece is not that type), implemented by zeroing entries of Pub_i and Pub_{−i}; it "seems to improve the performance in matches against humans" but "do[es] not significantly improve the winrate when DeepNash is evaluated against a version of itself". — [DeepNash, "Memory heuristic", "Value bounds heuristic" pp. 47–49](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Test-time post-processing: thresholding (drop actions with p < t_res = 0.03, renormalise) and discretisation (round to multiples of 1/n_disc; 32 in deployment, 16 in play); eagerness α_eag = 2.0 reshapes the no-attack ratio r' = 1 − (1 − r)^{α_eag}; a value-bounds heuristic with two-step lookahead removes actions whose upper-bound value is below the state value by margin 0.05 — it "affected less than 1.5% of DeepNash's turns" on Gravon. — [DeepNash, "Test Time Improvements" pp. 45–49, Table 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Results: vs humans on Gravon (April 2022) "roughly 50 ranked matches", 42 won (84%), rating 1799 (3rd in 2022 challenge ranking; top two 1868, 1831) and 1778 all-time (3rd; top two 1876, 1823). Vs bots (Table 1): Probe 100% (30 games), Master of the Flag 100% (30), Demon of Ignorance 97.1% (800), Asmodeus 99.7%, Celsius 98.2%, Celsius1.1 97.9%, PeternLewis 99.9%, Vixen 100% (800 each). — [DeepNash, Sec. 3.1–3.2, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Contrast — AlphaStar's critic *is* privileged: "To reduce variance, during training only, the value function is estimated using information from both the player's and the opponent's perspectives"; Methods: "we also use the opponent's observations as input to the value functions (ablation in Fig. 3K)"; Fig. 3K: average win-rate 82% with opponent info vs 22% without. — [AlphaStar, main text and Methods "Value and policy updates", Fig. 3K](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Contrast — AlphaStar's policy is recurrent over the full history: π_θ(a_t | s_t, z) with s_t = (o_{1:t}, a_{1:t−1}); "To deal with partial observability, the temporal sequence of observations is processed by a deep long short-term memory (LSTM) system". — [AlphaStar, "Learning Algorithm", "Architecture"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)

### Inferences
- (Inference) The DeepNash observation design is the key transferable trick: instead of a learned belief or a recurrent core, compute cheap, policy-independent public-information features (what each side can logically deduce) and feed them to both policy and value. For Fog-of-War chess the analogue is per-square "could contain piece type X" masks derived from legal-move consistency, plus the opponent's view of your own pieces (Pub_i), which DeepNash reports as mainly useful for "avoiding some types of inconsistent bluffing".
- (Inference) The paper's infrastructure section mentions recomputing "the exact network (LSTM) state", while the architecture section describes only convolutional blocks; this suggests the pipeline was built for recurrent nets but the final DeepNash torso was feed-forward over a 40-move window. Treat the exact presence of an LSTM as unresolved.
- (Inference) DeepNash's compute (1024 TPU nodes, 7.21M steps × 768 games × up to 3600 moves ≈ 10^13 agent steps upper bound) is 5–6 orders of magnitude beyond a single-GPU, 800-decisions/s setup; only the algorithm, not the scale, transfers.

### Gaps
- The paper does not report wall-clock training time, total environment steps actually consumed, or actor count in processes (only "256 TPU nodes for Actors").
- No ablation isolates the contribution of the public-information tensors vs. the 40-move history, nor of the non-privileged vs. a privileged critic (DeepNash never tried a privileged critic as far as the text shows).
- Whether the Science version differs materially from arXiv v1 was not checked (the Science article is paywalled); the DeepMind blog was not consulted.

## Key question 3: What league composition does AlphaStar use (main agents, main exploiters, league exploiters, PFSP) and what evidence shows it prevents cycling?

### Takeaway
AlphaStar's league = per race one main agent (35% self-play, 50% PFSP vs all past players, 15% PFSP vs forgotten/exploiter players; snapshot every 2·10⁹ steps; never reset), one main exploiter (trains only against current main agents; reset to supervised params each time it is added) and two league exploiters (PFSP vs whole league; 25% reset chance). PFSP samples opponents ∝ f(P[win]) with f_hard(x) = (1−x)^p (focus on hardest) or f_var(x) = x(1−x) (curriculum). Evidence against cycling is indirect but consistent: the league Nash puts weight only on recent players; main-agent payoffs are transitive (≈200 RPS cycles among main agents vs ≈3,000,000 involving exploiters); naive SP has similar Elo but much lower min win-rate vs past versions (46% vs 71%); adding exploiters raises Elo 1540→1824 and relative population performance 6%→62%.

### Cited Findings
- Motivation: FSP "avoids cycles by computing a best response against a uniform mixture of all previous policies; the mixture converges to a Nash equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games. We extend this approach to compute a best response against a non-uniform mixture of opponents." — [AlphaStar, "Learning Algorithm"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Three agent types: "main agents utilize a prioritized fictitious self-play (PFSP) mechanism that adapts the mixture probabilities proportionally to the win rate of each opponent against the agent ... With fixed probability, a main agent is selected as an opponent; this recovers the rapid learning of self-play. Second, main exploiter agents play only against the current iteration of main agents. Their purpose is to identify potential exploits in the main agents ... Third, league exploiter agents use a similar PFSP mechanism to the main agents, but are not targeted by main exploiter agents. Their purpose is to find systemic weaknesses of the entire league. Both main exploiters and league exploiters are periodically reinitialized". — [AlphaStar, "Learning Algorithm"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- PFSP formula: opponent B sampled from candidate set C with probability f(P[A beats B]) / Σ_{C'} f(P[A beats C']); "f_hard(x) = (1 − x)^p makes PFSP focus on the hardest players, where p ∈ R_+ controls how entropic the resulting distribution is. Since f_hard(1) = 0, no games are played against opponents that the agent already beats." "one can view f_hard as a form of smooth approximation of max-min optimisation, as opposed to max-avg that FSP imposes. In particular, this helps with integrating information from exploits, as these are strong but rare counter strategies, and a uniform mixture would be able to just ignore them". Alternative curriculum "f_var(x) = x(1 − x), where the agent preferentially plays against opponents around its own level. We use this curriculum for main exploiters and struggling main agents." — [AlphaStar, Methods "Prioritised Fictitious Self-Play"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Main agents: "trained with a proportion of 35% SP, 50% PFSP against all past players in the League, and an additional 15% of PFSP matches against forgotten main players the agent can no longer beat and past main exploiters. If there are no forgotten players or strong exploiters, the 15% is used for self-play instead. Every 2·10⁹ steps, a copy of the agent is added as a new player to the League. Main agents never reset." — [AlphaStar, Methods "Populating the League"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- League exploiters: "trained using PFSP and their frozen copies are added to the League when they defeat all players in the League in more than 70% of games, or after a timeout of 2·10⁹ steps. At this point there is a 25% probability that the agent is reset to the supervised parameters." — [AlphaStar, Methods](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Main exploiters: "play against main agents. Half of the time, and if the current probability of winning is lower than 20%, exploiters use PFSP with f_var weighting over players created by the main agents ... Otherwise ... it plays against the current main agents. These agents are added to the League whenever all three main agents are defeated in more than 70% of games, or after a timeout of 4·10⁹ steps. They are then reset to the supervised parameters." — [AlphaStar, Methods](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Composition and scale: "three main agents (one for each StarCraft race), three main exploiter agents (one for each race), and six league exploiter agents (two for each race). Each agent was trained using 32 third-generation tensor processing units (TPUs) over 44 days. During league training almost 900 distinct players were created." Per training agent: "16,000 concurrent StarCraft II matches and 16 actor tasks (each using a TPU v3 device with 8 TPU cores)"; "central 128-core TPU learner"; data "replayed twice"; batch 512; "about 50,000 agent steps per second"; actors refresh parameters every 10 s; "12 separate copies of this actor-learner setup"; a coordinator "maintains an estimate of the payoff matrix, samples new matches on request, and resets main and league exploiters." — [AlphaStar, main text; Methods "Infrastructure"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Evidence 1 (ablation, Fig. 3C/D; simplified setup: one map, PvP, 10^10 steps, main agents only, 50/50 SP+PFSP unless stated): Test Elo — pFSP+SP 1540, SP 1519, pFSP 1273, FSP 1143; min win-rate vs. all past versions (averaged over time, "a proxy for forgetting") — pFSP+SP 71%, SP 46%, pFSP 70%, FSP 69%. "Naive self-play has high Elo, but is more forgetful." — [AlphaStar, Fig. 3C/D and caption](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Evidence 2 (league composition, Fig. 3A/B): Elo of main agents — main agents only 1540, + main exploiters 1693, + league exploiters 1824; Relative Population Performance (which "measures exploitability") — 6%, 35%, 62% respectively. — [AlphaStar, Fig. 3A/B](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Evidence 3 (Nash of the league over time, Fig. 4C): "The Nash distribution (mixture of the least exploitable players) of the players in the League, as training progressed. It puts the most weight on recent players, suggesting that latest strategies largely dominate the previous ones, without much forgetting or cycling. For example, player 40 was part of the Nash since its creation at day 20, until 5 days later, when it was completely dominated by newer agents." Main text: "The league Nash equilibrium over all players at each point in time assigns small probabilities to players from previous iterations, suggesting that the learning algorithm does not cycle or regress." Also "The performance of the main exploiters actually reduced over time". — [AlphaStar, main text "Empirical Evaluation", Fig. 4C](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Evidence 4 (payoff structure, Extended Data Fig. 8): "The main agents behave transitively: the more recent agents win consistently against older main agents and exploiters. Interactions between exploiters are highly non-transitive – across the full payoff, there are around 3,000,000 rock-paper-scissor cycles (with requirement of at least 70% win rates to form a cycle) that involve at least one exploiter, and around 200 that involve only main agents." — [AlphaStar, Extended Data Fig. 8](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Evidence 5 (Extended Data Fig. 5): "PFSP based training outperforms FSP under all measures considered: it has a stronger population measured by relative population performance, provides a less exploitable solution, and has better final agent performance against the corresponding League." — [AlphaStar, Extended Data Fig. 5](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Evidence 6: "Proportion of Validation Agents [17 strategies trained with hand-chosen z] that beat the main agents in more than 80 out of 160 games ... increased steadily over time, which shows the robustness of League training to unseen strategies." (Fig. 4B) — [AlphaStar, Fig. 4B, Methods "Analysis"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- RL inside the league: win/loss terminal reward "(−1 on a loss, 0 on a draw and +1 on a win) ... without a discount"; actor-critic with V-trace for the policy, TD(λ) for the value, UPGO self-imitation, KL toward the supervised policy, entropy regularisation, Adam; off-policy from a replay buffer. The RL "is designed to maximize the win rate (that is, compute a best response) against a mixture of opponents" — i.e. the league, not the RL update, carries the equilibrium-seeking burden. — [AlphaStar, "Learning Algorithm"; Methods "Reinforcement Learning"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Relation to PSRO: "the Policy Space Response Oracle framework is related to League training, although League training specifies unique targets for approximate best responses (i.e. PFSP and exploiters)." — [AlphaStar, Methods "Related Work"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Caveat on evaluation: Battle.net matches "do not directly measure AlphaStar's susceptibility to exploitation under repeated play." Final MMR 6,275 (Protoss), 6,048 (Terran), 5,835 (Zerg), "above 99.8% of ranked human players". — [AlphaStar, main text](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)

### Inferences
- (Inference) AlphaStar's anti-cycling evidence is population-level and indirect (Nash support, transitivity counts, min-win-rate vs past), not an exploitability measurement of the final policy; a Fog-of-War chess project can measure exploitability more directly (DQN/PPO best-response training as MMD does) and should.
- (Inference) The exploiters' role is specifically to generate the "strong but rare counter strategies" that uniform FSP would dilute; f_hard weighting ensures the main agent cannot ignore them. For a one-GPU league the cheapest version is: periodic frozen snapshots + f_hard-weighted sampling + one exploiter slot that is reset to the current main agent (there is no supervised init in a from-scratch setup, which removes AlphaStar's diversity source — a real risk).
- (Inference) AlphaStar's 35/50/15 split and the 70% / 2·10⁹-step snapshot rules are tuned for StarCraft at ~50k steps/s per learner; the structure, not the constants, is what to copy.

### Gaps
- The Nature paper reports no ablation isolating the 35/50/15 proportions, the p in f_hard, or the exploiter reset probability (25%).
- No exploitability-vs-compute curve for the league; RPP is only reported for the simplified ablation setup.
- The supplementary pseudocode (pseudocode.zip) and detailed-architecture.txt were not retrieved.

## Key question 4 (supporting): NFSP and PSRO — algorithms, assumptions, guarantees, scaling evidence, compute cost and failure modes

### Takeaway
NFSP = two networks per player (DQN best-response + supervised average-policy from a reservoir buffer) mixed by anticipatory parameter η = 0.1; converges (in the average policy) via fictitious play theory, is off-policy/replay based, and reached competitive-with-superhuman-bots play in Limit Hold'em at ~35M iterations with four-layer MLPs. PSRO = population of frozen policies + empirical payoff matrix + meta-solver (Nash/PRD/uniform) + an RL oracle trained each epoch against the meta-mixture; generalises FP/DO/InRL, converges like double oracle in two-player games but worst-case must enumerate the strategy space, and each epoch costs a full RL run (25k→250k episodes per epoch, 20 epochs, in Leduc).

### Cited Findings

**NFSP**
- Algorithm 1: per episode set σ ← ε-greedy(Q) with probability η, Π with probability 1−η; store (s_t, a_t, r_{t+1}, s_{t+1}) in M_RL (circular buffer); if following the best response, store (s_t, a_t) in M_SL (reservoir); update θ_Π by SGD on L(θ_Π) = E_{(s,a)∼M_SL}[−log Π(s,a|θ_Π)]; update θ_Q on the DQN loss E[(r + max_{a'} Q(s',a'|θ_Q') − Q(s,a|θ_Q))²]; periodically copy the target network. — [NFSP, Alg. 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01121)
- Why reservoir sampling and anticipatory dynamics: "it uses reservoir sampling (Vitter, 1985) to avoid windowing artifacts due to sampling from a finite memory. Second, it uses anticipatory dynamics (Shamma and Arslan, 2005)"; agents "choose their actions from the mixture policy σ ≡ (1 − η)π̂ + ηβ̂"; this lets each agent compute "an approximate best response ... to its opponents' anticipated average strategy profile" while still generating best-response data for the SL memory. — [NFSP, Sec. 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01121)
- Theory/assumptions: "The average strategies of fictitious players converge to Nash equilibria in certain classes of games, e.g. two-player zero-sum and many-player potential games"; generalised weakened fictitious play "allows for approximate best responses and perturbed average strategy updates"; extensive-form averaging uses realization-equivalent behavioural mixing σ(s,a) ∝ λ₁x_{π₁}(s)π₁(s,a) + λ₂x_{π₂}(s)π₂(s,a) (Eq. 1). "We assume games with perfect recall". Off-policy: "trains a neural network, Q(s, a | θ_Q), to predict action values from data in M_RL using off-policy reinforcement learning". — [NFSP, Secs. 2.2–2.3, 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01121)
- Leduc hyperparameters: 1 hidden layer of 64 ReLU; M_RL 200k (circular), M_SL 2m (reservoir); RL lr 0.1, SL lr 0.005, vanilla SGD; 2 updates of minibatch 128 per network per 128 game steps; target network refit every 300 updates; η = 0.1; ε from 0.06 decayed to 0 ∝ 1/√iterations. Result: exploitability 0.06, "which full-width XFP typically achieves after around 1000 full-width iterations". Ablations: "using a fixed-size sliding window to store experience of the agents' own behaviour led to divergence"; η = 0.5 "plateaued"; exponentially-averaged reservoir "led to noisy performance". — [NFSP, Sec. 4.1, Fig. 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01121)
- Limit Hold'em scale: four hidden layers 1024/512/1024/512; M_RL 600k, M_SL 30m (exponentially-averaged reservoir, min replacement prob 0.25); lr 0.1 / 0.01; 2 updates of batch 256 per 256 steps; target every 1000 updates; η = 0.1; ε 0.08 → 0; ~35M iterations on the x-axis of Fig. 2a. Greedy-average strategy vs ACPC 2014 top-3: escabeche −52.1±8.5, SmooCT −17.4±9.0, Hyperborean −13.6±9.2 mbb/h; "the top half of computer agents in the ACPC 2014 achieved up to 50 mbb/h between themselves"; "expert human players typically achieve expected win rates of 40–60 mbb/h". — [NFSP, Sec. 4.3, Fig. 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01121)
- OpenSpiel PyTorch NFSP defaults: `batch_size=128, rl_learning_rate=0.01, sl_learning_rate=0.01, min_buffer_size_to_learn=1000, learn_every=64, optimizer_str="sgd"`; `reservoir_buffer_capacity` and `anticipatory_param` are required arguments; mode is resampled per episode (`BEST_RESPONSE` with prob anticipatory_param, else `AVERAGE_POLICY`); the RL agent is only trained (`_rl_agent.learn()`) in AVERAGE_POLICY mode. — [OpenSpiel pytorch/nfsp.py](https://github.com/google-deepmind/open_spiel/blob/master/open_spiel/python/pytorch/nfsp.py)
- PSRO paper's tuned NFSP values for 2-player Leduc NashConv: RL lr 1e-3, SL lr 1e-5, anticipatory 0.1, reservoir 100000. — [PSRO, App. C.2.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Known weaknesses (as stated by others): DeepNash — best-response methods "remain memory-intensive because they potentially require an exponential ... number of different policies ... which, combined with the time required to compute a best-response, also makes them very slow." MMD — NFSP/PSRO "can require computing many best responses ... making it very slow in some cases"; in deep experiments NFSP's approximate exploitability after 10M steps was 59±1 / 78±5 (×10⁻²) vs MMD's 20±1 / 15±1, though the authors caution the OpenSpiel NFSP defaults are likely weaker than RLlib PPO defaults. — [DeepNash Related Work](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378); [MMD App. J, K, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)

**PSRO**
- Algorithm 1: initial policy sets Π (start "with a single policy (uniform random)"); compute expected utilities U^Π for each joint policy; meta-strategies σ_i = UNIFORM; each epoch, for each player, "for many episodes: Sample π_{−i} ∼ σ_{−i}; Train oracle π'_i over ρ ∼ (π'_i, π_{−i})"; add π'_i to Π_i; "Compute missing entries in U^Π"; "Compute a meta-strategy σ from U^Π". "when the other players are fixed the environment becomes Markovian and computing a best response reduces to solving a form of MDP"; evaluating the empirical tensor "takes time exponential in |Π|" (in number of players). — [PSRO, Sec. 3, Alg. 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Special cases: iterated best response σ_{−i} = (0,…,1,0); independent RL (0,…,0,1); fictitious play (1/K,…,1/K,0); "Double Oracle is an instance of PSRO with n = 2 and σ^T set to a Nash equilibrium profile of the meta-game". Trade-off: FP "can only sharpen the meta-strategy distribution by repeatedly generating the same best responses"; DO responses "overfit to a specific equilibrium" and in zero-sum "be unable to generalize to parts of the space not reached by any equilibrium strategy"; compromise: "meta-strategies with full support that force (mix in) γ exploration". — [PSRO, Sec. 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Meta-solvers: regret matching, Hedge, and projected replicator dynamics (PRD) dx_k/dt = x_k[(Ay)_k − xᵀAy] with projection onto the γ-exploratory simplex {x | x_k ≥ γ/(K+1)}; step δ = 0.01, running averages of 50/10 values (App. C.5). Best settings in Leduc: "γ = 0.4 works best for minimizing NashConv"; "decoupled replicator dynamics works best, followed by decoupled regret-matching and Exp3"; "the higher the level, the lower the resulting NashConv value is, with diminishing improvements." — [PSRO, Secs. 3.1, 4.2, App. A, C.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Convergence: "DO is guaranteed to converge to an equilibrium in two-player games. But, in the worst-case, the entire strategy space may have to be enumerated. For example, this is necessary for Rock-Paper-Scissors, whose only equilibrium has full support (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)." — [PSRO, Sec. 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Assumptions: "we assume centralized training for decentralized execution: policies are represented as separate neural networks and there is no sharing of gradients nor architectures among agents"; no symmetry assumption (per-player populations). Oracle = Reactor (Retrace(λ) off-policy evaluation, β-LOO policy gradient, recurrent), replay 500000, lr 1e-4, Adam β₁ = 0. — [PSRO, Secs. 1, 4, App. C.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- DCH (parallel approximation): fixed K levels, nK processes, each trains one oracle π_{i,k} against σ_{−i,k} and updates its meta-strategy online with decoupled (bandit) meta-solvers; space O(n²K²) vs K^n payoff tensor; "trades away accuracy of the correspondence to PSRO for practical efficiency". — [PSRO, Sec. 3.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Joint policy correlation (InRL overfitting): on Laser Tag small2, independently trained pairs lose R⁻ = 0.342 of reward when cross-played (D̄ = 30.44, Ō = 20.03); small4 R⁻ = 0.717; DCH (level 10) reduces to 0.055 and 0.150. "The problem gets larger as the map size grows and problem becomes more partially observed". — [PSRO, Sec. 4.1, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Leduc comparison: "DCH (and PSRO) converge faster than NFSP at the start of training ... and NFSP converges to a lower exploitability in later episodes. We believe that this is due to NFSP's ability to learn a more accurate mixed average strategy at states far down in the tree ... whereas DCH and PSRO mix at the top over full policies." PSRO/DCH "achieve higher performance against the fixed players ... computing a robust counter-strategy". Reference: vanilla CFR NashConv at 500 iterations = 0.063591 (2-player). Note the Leduc variant used is harder (illegal action → reward −14 and random legal move). — [PSRO, Sec. 4.2, App. D.1, E.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Compute cost per oracle (2-player Leduc PSRO, App. C.3.1): lr 5e-4, batch 16, replay 5e5, unroll 2, entropy cost 0.1, exploration decay end 250000 steps, target update 1000, meta-game update frequency 2500, episodes per epoch linearly 25000 → 2.5e5, maximum epochs 20; "the most important parameter was the learning rate" (sensitivity 4/5). — [PSRO, App. C.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- OpenSpiel PSRO v2 constructor: `meta_strategy_method="alpharank"` default, `sims_per_entry` (simulations per payoff-matrix entry) required, `rectifier` option ("rectified": train only against strategies beaten by current strategy), `training_strategy_selector` ("top_k_probabilites" / "probabilistic" / "exhaustive"). — [OpenSpiel psro_v2.py](https://github.com/google-deepmind/open_spiel/blob/master/open_spiel/python/algorithms/psro_v2/psro_v2.py)
- PSRO on a Stratego variant: "Pipeline PSRO (20) was able to beat these handcrafted bots in Barrage Stratego (by at most 81% win-rate), but didn't show results against top human players." — [DeepNash, "Work on Barrage Stratego"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)

### Inferences
- (Inference) NFSP's two-network/two-buffer design is simple to bolt onto an existing DQN but not onto a PPO loop (it needs an off-policy Q-learner for the best response); PSRO needs a full RL run per epoch (20 epochs × up to 250k episodes even in Leduc) and a payoff matrix of |Π|² evaluations, which is the cost item that scales worst on one GPU.
- (Inference) The PSRO paper's observation that mixing "at the top over full policies" loses precision deep in the tree is directly relevant to Fog-of-War chess (long games, mixing matters per-infostate), and favours R-NaD/MMD (per-infostate regularised policy) or NFSP (behavioural average) over a PSRO meta-mixture as the final policy.

### Gaps
- No NFSP or PSRO result exists on a chess-sized imperfect-information game in these papers; the largest is Limit Hold'em (NFSP) and Barrage Stratego (Pipeline PSRO, cited second-hand).
- PSRO's paper does not report wall-clock or GPU-hours per epoch.

## Key question 5: For one GPU and a Python simulator at ~800 decisions/s, which method is realistically implementable, and what is the minimum viable R-NaD or MMD on top of an existing PPO loop?

### Takeaway
MMD is the only one of these methods that the primary literature shows as a literal one-term modification of PPO (reverse-KL penalty + entropy/magnet term + a 1/√t schedule), and it was validated at 1M–10M environment steps on small imperfect-information games — roughly 20 minutes to 3.5 hours of simulation at 800 decisions/s. R-NaD adds the same regulariser inside the reward plus a frozen reference network swapped every Δ steps; its OpenSpiel defaults (batch 256, 20k steps/iteration) are single-machine scale. NFSP is feasible but needs a Q-learning branch; PSRO and a full AlphaStar league are compute-dominated by best-response/oracle training and are best used as a light "frozen checkpoint league" layer on top of an MMD/R-NaD learner, not as the core.

### Cited Findings
- Deep MMD was run for 1M and 10M time steps in self-play, 5 seeds, with exploitability measured by a DQN best response trained for 10M steps; MMD's approximate exploitability after only 1M steps (34±2 / 37±1 ×10⁻²) was already below every NFSP or PPO seed at 10M steps. — [MMD, App. J, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Exact deep-MMD recipe: PPO (RLlib) with "the adaptive forward KL regularization" replaced by "a constant reverse KL regularization", η_t = α_t = 0.05·sqrt(10⁷/t) (t = env steps), other hyperparameters default. — [MMD, App. J](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Loss form to implement: E_t[(π/π_old)Â_t + α H(π(s_t)) − β KL(π(s_t), π_old(s_t))], β = 1/η; with a non-uniform magnet the entropy term generalises to −α KL(π, ρ) (Eq. 10). Alternative Euclidean (logit-space) form: z_{t+1} = [z_t + η∇_z E_{softmax(z)}q + αηζ]/(1+αη) (App. H.7), which "also exhibits convergence behavior in Leduc poker. However, convergence may be slower than the negative entropy variant." — [MMD, App. L, Eq. 10, App. H.7](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Moving-magnet alternative to annealing: ρ_{t+1} ∝ ρ_t^{1−η̃} π_{t+1}^{η̃} (an EMA in log-probability space), α = 1, η = 0.1, η̃ = 0.05 in tabular tests; convergence "may occur at a much faster rate" than annealing. — [MMD, App. H.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- R-NaD minimal loop (from the paper and OpenSpiel): (1) keep two frozen reference parameter sets; (2) add to the acting player's reward −η log(π(a_t|o_t)/π_reg(a_t|o_t)) (and +η for the opponent), with π_reg = α-blend of the two references, α = min(1, 2n/Δ); (3) every Δ learner steps, set reference ← target/EMA network, old reference ← previous reference; (4) critic from full-trajectory V-trace on transformed rewards; (5) policy update by NeuRD on logits with β-band (β = 2) or, per the paper's NFG section, any replicator/FoReL-equivalent RL update. Defaults: η = 0.2, Δ = 20k (OpenSpiel) / 10k–100k (DeepNash), target EMA 0.001, lr 5e-5, Adam b1 = 0. — [DeepNash pp. 28–30, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378); [OpenSpiel rnad.py](https://github.com/google-deepmind/open_spiel/blob/v1.5/open_spiel/python/algorithms/rnad/rnad.py)
- The reward in all three large-scale references is exact win/loss: DeepNash uses per-step game rewards with forced draws; AlphaStar "(−1 on a loss, 0 on a draw and +1 on a win) as the terminal reward r_T, without a discount". — [DeepNash, Sec. 2.3.1; AlphaStar Methods "Reinforcement Learning"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Evidence that plain PPO self-play will not reduce exploitability: Table 2 of MMD (PPO 10M steps: 100±0 and 90±4 ×10⁻²; worse than uniform random). — [MMD, App. J](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05825)
- Compute reference points for the other methods: DeepNash — 768 + 256 TPU nodes, 7.21M learner steps × 768 full games; AlphaStar — 12 × (32 TPU v3 + 16,000 concurrent games), 44 days, ~50k agent steps/s per learner; PSRO (Leduc) — 20 epochs × 25k→250k episodes per oracle plus payoff-matrix simulation; NFSP (LHE) — ~35M iterations with 600k/30M buffers. — [DeepNash Table 2, p. 42](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378); [AlphaStar Methods "Infrastructure"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf); [PSRO App. C.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832); [NFSP Sec. 4.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.01121)
- Post-processing is cheap and was needed even at DeepNash scale: thresholding at 0.03 and discretisation to 1/32 (deploy) or 1/16 (play) because softmax "assigns a non-zero probability to every action" and "an opponent who prolongs the game ... will eventually benefit from one of these low-probability errors." — [DeepNash, "Fine-tuning", "Post-processing of policy"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Privileged critic is a documented large win where available: AlphaStar Fig. 3K 82% vs 22% average win-rate with vs without opponent information in the value function, used "only during training". — [AlphaStar, Fig. 3K](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)

### Inferences (practical plan; all inferences)
- Throughput budget: 800 decisions/s ≈ 2.9M decisions/hour ≈ 69M/day. The MMD deep experiments' 10M-step regime is ~3.5 h of simulation; a Fog-of-War chess game is far longer than Dark Hex (tens to hundreds of decisions), so expect to need 10⁸–10⁹ decisions (days to weeks) for anything resembling convergence; the simulator, not the GPU, is the bottleneck, which argues for the cheapest learner-side method (MMD on PPO) and against population methods that multiply simulation cost by |Π|.
- Minimum viable MMD on an existing PPO loop (what changes): (1) remove or keep the clip, but add `beta * KL(pi_new || pi_old)` (reverse direction: expectation under the *new* policy) to the policy loss; (2) add `alpha * H(pi_new)` (uniform magnet) or `-alpha * KL(pi_new || rho)` with a frozen magnet network ρ; (3) schedule α and 1/β as c·sqrt(T0/t) (MMD) or hold them fixed and update ρ as a slow EMA of the policy (moving magnet); (4) keep the exact win/loss reward; (5) at evaluation, threshold tiny probabilities (DeepNash's 0.03) before sampling. Nothing else in PPO (GAE, value loss, minibatching) needs to change, which is exactly what Sokota et al. did with RLlib.
- Minimum viable R-NaD on PPO: same as above but (1) move the KL term into the reward as an intrinsic per-step term r_t − η(log π(a_t|o_t) − log π_reg(a_t|o_t)) for the mover and +η(...) for the opponent (so the critic learns the regularised value); (2) hold π_reg as a frozen copy, replace it every Δ updates with the current (EMA) policy and linearly blend old/new references over the first half of the next period; (3) optionally swap the PPO surrogate for a NeuRD/logit update with the β = 2 band. This is more invasive than MMD because the reward transform must be applied inside advantage estimation (GAE over transformed rewards), but it matches the only recipe proven at expert human level.
- Partial observability on one GPU: copy DeepNash's input design (policy-independent "possible piece types per square" planes for both sides, k-move history planes, draw/no-progress scalars) rather than a belief model; add a privileged critic that sees the true board during training (AlphaStar evidence) since the simulator exposes true state at zero cost — this departs from DeepNash but is supported by AlphaStar's ablation.
- League layer: keep a small pool of frozen checkpoints and sample opponents with f_hard(x) = (1−x)^p weighting (AlphaStar PFSP) for a fraction of games (AlphaStar's 50%), the rest pure self-play; add one exploiter slot periodically reset from the main agent. This costs no extra GPU training if the exploiter shares the learner budget, but every league game is a simulator game, so the pool should stay small (tens, not hundreds). Measure exploitability with an occasional dedicated best-response run (MMD's DQN-BR protocol) rather than relying on Elo.
- NFSP is implementable (OpenSpiel PyTorch reference, small MLPs) but requires a DQN branch and reservoir SL buffer alongside PPO; given MMD's head-to-head results against OpenSpiel-default NFSP it is a lower-priority baseline. Full PSRO and a full AlphaStar league are not realistic as the core method at 800 decisions/s because oracle training multiplies simulation cost per epoch.

### Gaps
- No paper tests MMD or R-NaD on a game with chess-like move counts and a Python-speed simulator; the required number of environment steps for Fog-of-War chess is unknown and must be measured.
- MMD's deep experiments did not use legal-action masking in the PPO/MMD agents (RLlib wrapper limitation, App. J) and did not report a sweep over α/η, so the 0.05·sqrt(10M/t) schedule is a single working point, not a tuned recommendation.
- No published evidence on combining a regularised learner (R-NaD/MMD) with a PFSP league; whether the two interact well (e.g. whether the league's non-stationarity breaks the fixed-point argument) is untested in the sources reviewed.
- DeepMind blog / Science version of DeepNash and the AlphaStar supplementary pseudocode were not consulted; hyperparameters above come from the arXiv/unformatted PDFs and OpenSpiel code.
