# Policy-gradient methods for Fog-of-War chess (recurrent actor, privileged simulator, exact +1/-1/0 terminal reward)

Scope of these notes: primary-source reading (arXiv PDFs, full text) of PPO, GRPO (DeepSeekMath), RLOO ("Back to Basics"), VinePPO, asymmetric actor-critic (Pinto et al.), unbiased asymmetric actor-critic (Baisero & Amato), and recurrent model-free RL for POMDPs (Ni et al.), plus the PPO-implementation literature (Engstrom et al. 2020; the ICLR-blog "37 implementation details"; Andrychowicz et al. 2020; OpenAI Spinning Up). ## Per-paper notes (core algorithm, assumptions, bias/variance, scaling evidence, failure modes)

### Takeaway
Three families emerge: (a) learned-critic methods (PPO; asymmetric and unbiased-asymmetric actor-critic; recurrent off-policy actor-critic in Ni et al.), which need value-function learning but only standard episodic rollouts; (b) critic-free group/leave-one-out baselines (GRPO, RLOO), which need K i.i.d. samples from the same initial state and treat the whole trajectory as one action; (c) critic-free Monte-Carlo state-value estimation (VinePPO), which needs the simulator to reset to arbitrary intermediate states. Only Baisero & Amato addresses when a privileged (true-state) critic is a valid baseline under partial observability.

### Cited Findings

#### PPO (Schulman et al. 2017, arXiv 1707.06347)
- Probability ratio r_t(θ) = π_θ(a_t|s_t)/π_θold(a_t|s_t); clipped surrogate L^CLIP(θ) = Ê_t[ min( r_t(θ) Â_t, clip(r_t(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε) Â_t ) ], with ε = 0.2 suggested (Sec. 3, Eq. 7) — [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)
- Adaptive-KL alternative: L^KLPEN(θ) = Ê_t[ r_t(θ) Â_t − β KL[π_θold(·|s_t), π_θ(·|s_t)] ]; after each update compute d = Ê_t[KL]; if d < d_targ/1.5 then β ← β/2; if d > d_targ×1.5 then β ← 2β; "we found that the KL penalty performed worse than the clipped surrogate objective" (Sec. 4, Eq. 8) — [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)
- Combined loss when policy and value share parameters: L^CLIP+VF+S = Ê_t[ L^CLIP_t − c1 L^VF_t + c2 S[π_θ](s_t) ], L^VF_t = (V_θ(s_t) − V_t^targ)^2; Atari uses c1 = 1, c2 = 0.01 (Sec. 5, Eq. 9; Table 5) — [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)
- Advantage: truncated GAE over a T-step segment, Â_t = δ_t + (γλ)δ_{t+1} + ... + (γλ)^{T−t+1} δ_{T−1}, δ_t = r_t + γV(s_{t+1}) − V(s_t) (Sec. 5, Eqs. 11–12) — [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)
- Algorithm 1: N parallel actors each run π_θold for T timesteps, compute Â_1..Â_T, then K epochs of minibatch SGD (minibatch M ≤ NT) on the surrogate; data is on-policy per iteration and reused only within the K epochs (Sec. 5) — [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)
- MuJoCo 1M-step benchmark hyperparameters (Table 3): horizon T=2048, Adam stepsize 3e-4, 10 epochs, minibatch 64, γ=0.99, λ=0.95. Atari (Table 5): T=128, stepsize 2.5e-4×α, 3 epochs, minibatch 32×8, 8 actors, clip 0.1×α, c1=1, c2=0.01, with α annealed linearly 1→0 — [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)
- Surrogate ablation (Table 1, avg normalized score over 7 MuJoCo envs × 21 runs): no clipping/penalty −0.39; clipping ε=0.1 0.76, ε=0.2 0.82, ε=0.3 0.70; adaptive KL d_targ=0.003/0.01/0.03 → 0.68/0.74/0.71; fixed KL β=0.3/1/3/10 → 0.62/0.71/0.72/0.69 (Sec. 6.1) — [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)
- Needs value-function learning: yes (V_θ for GAE). Simulator needs: ordinary episodic rollouts from the environment's own start distribution; no resets to arbitrary states, no privileged state (the paper is MDP-only) — [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)

#### GRPO (Shao et al. 2024, DeepSeekMath, arXiv 2402.03300)
- Motivation for dropping the critic: "the value function employed in PPO is typically another model of comparable size as the policy model, it brings a substantial memory and computational burden" and, because "usually only the last token is assigned a reward score by the reward model", training a per-token-accurate value function is complicated (Sec. 4.1.1) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- Objective (Eq. 3): for each question q sample a group {o_1..o_G} ~ π_θold(·|q); J_GRPO(θ) = E[ (1/G) Σ_i (1/|o_i|) Σ_t ( min( ρ_{i,t} Â_{i,t}, clip(ρ_{i,t},1−ε,1+ε) Â_{i,t} ) − β D_KL[π_θ || π_ref] ) ], ρ_{i,t} = π_θ(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t}) / π_θold(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t}). The KL is added to the loss rather than to the reward "avoiding complicating the calculation of Â_{i,t}" (Sec. 4.1.1) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- KL estimator (Eq. 4): D_KL = π_ref(o_{i,t}|·)/π_θ(o_{i,t}|·) − log(π_ref(o_{i,t}|·)/π_θ(o_{i,t}|·)) − 1, "guaranteed to be positive" (Schulman 2020 estimator) (Sec. 4.1.1) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- Outcome supervision: rewards r = {r_1..r_G} are normalized by group mean and std, and every token of o_i gets the same advantage Â_{i,t} = (r_i − mean(r))/std(r) (Sec. 4.1.2) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- Process supervision: step rewards normalized by the mean/std over all steps of all G outputs; Â_{i,t} = sum of normalized rewards of steps ending at or after token t (Sec. 4.1.3) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- Gradient form (Appendix A.1.6, Eq. 20–21): ∇J = E[(1/G)Σ_i (1/|o_i|)Σ_t ( Â_{i,t} + β( π_ref/π_θ − 1 ) ) ∇ log π_θ(o_{i,t}|·)]; i.e. GRPO is REINFORCE with a group-normalized scalar coefficient plus a KL term — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- Iterative GRPO (Algorithm 1): reference model reset to current policy each iteration; reward model continually trained with a replay of 10% historical data (Sec. 4.1.4) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- Training hyperparameters: policy lr 1e-6, KL coefficient 0.04, 64 sampled outputs per question, max length 1024, batch 1024, and "the policy model only has a single update following each exploration stage" (so π_θold = π_θ in practice) (Sec. 4.2) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- Results: DeepSeekMath-RL 7B 88.2% GSM8K, 51.7% MATH (chain-of-thought), improving over DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B on all benchmarks (Sec. 4.2, Table 5) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- Failure/limitation reported: "RL enhances Maj@K but not Pass@K", i.e. it makes the output distribution more robust (reranks top-K) rather than enhancing fundamental capability (Sec. 5.2.2, Figure 7) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- Needs value-function learning: no. Simulator needs: ability to draw G independent samples from the same initial "state" (prompt q); no intermediate resets; no privileged information — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)

#### RLOO / "Back to Basics" (Ahmadian et al. 2024, arXiv 2402.14740)
- Objective: max E_{x~D, y~π_θ(·|x)}[ r_φ(x,y) − β D_KL(π_θ(·|x) || π_ref(·|x)) ] (Eq. 2); REINFORCE estimator on the whole completion as one action: E[ R(y,x) ∇_θ log π_θ(y|x) ] (Eq. 6), with baseline b (Eq. 7); moving-average baseline b_MA (Eq. 8) (Sec. 2.2) — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- RLOO estimator (Sec. 2.3): (1/k) Σ_{i=1..k} [ R(y^(i),x) − (1/(k−1)) Σ_{j≠i} R(y^(j),x) ] ∇ log π(y^(i)|x), for y^(1..k) i.i.d. ~ π_θ(·|x); "uses the remaining k−1 samples to create an unbiased estimate of the expected return for the prompt, akin to a parameter-free value-function, but estimated at each training step"; cost is "increased sampling time during training" — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Bandit argument (Sec. 3.3): the LLM environment is fully deterministic (P(y_{<t+1},x | s_t, y_t) = 1), so "the problem can be reduced to a bandit problem, where the MDP consists of only the initial state as determined by the prompt, and the terminal state" — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Variance/bias argument (Sec. 3.1): PPO bootstraps off a state-value function to lower variance at the price of bias; the authors "posit that this is not a practical concern in fine-tuning LLMs due to the extremely strong initialization of the policy"; GAE-λ sweep (Fig. 1) shows "monotonically decreasing reward with decreasing λ", λ = 1 (Vanilla PG, no bootstrapping) best — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Clipping (Sec. 3.2): "the loss is actually clipped on average < 5% of the time per batch"; turning off policy and value clipping "does not impact learning meaningfully"; removing clipping and the ratio (reducing to Vanilla PG) "if anything ... gives a slight boost" — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Results (Table 1, win-rates vs reference completions; TL;DR / HH-Pythia / HH-Llama): RLOO k=4 77.9/43.7/64.1; RAFT k=4 73.2/42.1/63.3; RLOO k=2 74.2/47.6/62.2; REINFORCE w/ baseline 70.7/37.9/55.3; Vanilla PG 70.4/36.4/52.3; PPO 67.6/29.2/32.0; DPO 66.6/39.0/61.9. "RLOO with k = 4 ... outperforming PPO by 10.3, 14.5, and 32.1" (Sec. 5.2) — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Partial-completion modelling unnecessary: REINFORCE-with-baseline (whole sequence) is on par with Vanilla PG (per-token) at 70.7 vs 70.4 and 37.9 vs 36.4 (Sec. 5.2) — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Robustness: RLOO shows a smaller reward drop than RAFT under injected reward-logit noise σ ∈ {1,3,5} (Fig. 6) and under larger KL β (Fig. 5); RLOO k=2 has ~27% less gradient variance than Vanilla PG while matching/outperforming it (Sec. 5.3/5.4) — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Setup/hyperparameters: Pythia-6.9B and Llama-7B; TL;DR and Anthropic-HH; constant lr 1e-6 with 3% warm-up; 2 gradient steps per batch; β = 0.10 for HH (Sec. 4.1, App. C) — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Needs value-function learning: no (Vanilla PG variant does learn a per-token baseline b_φ(s_t), Eq. 9). Simulator needs: k i.i.d. completions per prompt, i.e. k resets to the same initial state; nothing else — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)

#### VinePPO (Kazemnejad et al. 2024, arXiv 2410.01679)
- Core estimator (Sec. 4, Eq. 5–6): for each state s_t on a training trajectory, "re-feed the partial context corresponding to s_t to the current policy to sample K auxiliary rollouts η_1..η_K ~ π_θ(·|s_t)"; V̂_MC(s_t) = (1/K) Σ_k R(η_k); Â_MC(s_t,a_t) = r(s_t,a_t) + γ V̂_MC(s_{t+1}) − V̂_MC(s_t). "For any K ≥ 1, the policy gradient computed using ... Â_MC is an unbiased estimate of the gradient of expected return." Auxiliary rollouts are not used for policy-gradient updates — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- "States" are grouped into reasoning steps (newline-delimited) and one advantage is assigned to all tokens in a step (Sec. 4; App. B) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Reset assumption (Sec. 2 and 4): TRPO's "Vine" variant "is limited to environments that allow intermediate state resets, rare in typical RL settings. However, language generation ... enables such intermediate reset capabilities. In domains with similar reset capabilities, such as Go and Chess, MC-based methods like AlphaGo ... have emerged." Footnote 2: the derivation "is possible as the language environment is deterministic" — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Everything else is standard PPO: clipped loss with KL added to the loss (Eq. 8–10), λ = 1 and γ = 1 found best ("similar to the findings of Trung et al. (2024) and Ahmadian et al. (2024)"), so GAE reduces to Σ_{t'≥t} r_{t'} − V̂(s_t) (App. A) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Hyperparameters (Tables 1–2): 8 responses per prompt, 512 episodes per step, minibatch 64, 2 inner epochs, lr 1e-6, temperature 0.6, ε = 0.2, value-clip 0.2, KL β = 1e-4 for PPO/VinePPO and 3e-3 for GRPO/RLOO; K = 9 auxiliary rollouts by default (Sec. 5) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Results: VinePPO outperforms PPO, GRPO, RLOO, RestEM and DPO+ on Pass@1 for RhoMath 1.1B and DeepSeekMath 7B on GSM8K and MATH, with the gap widening on MATH (Fig. 4, Sec. 6.1); reaches PPO's peak accuracy in up to 3.0x less wall-clock time and "9x and 2.8x fewer steps compared to PPO" (1.1B and 7B respectively) (Sec. 6.2, Fig. 6) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Cost: per-iteration time 80s (PPO) vs 380s (VinePPO) for 1.1B on 4×A100; 312s vs 583s for 7B on 8×H100 (Table 5); "PPO uses double GPU memory — the value network" (Sec. 6.2) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- K ablation (Fig. 7): accuracy increases monotonically with K ∈ {1,3,9} on RhoMath 1.1B/MATH; "even small K values work well"; larger K also improves compute efficiency (Sec. 6.4) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Why value networks fail (Sec. 7, ground truth = 256-sample MC): PPO's value network had explained variance 0.7–0.9 (so not undertrained) yet its predictions "within 0.05 of the ground truth" start low and only reach ~65%, vs 70–90% for VinePPO; PPO's value net is "near chance" at identifying the best of five candidate next steps (Fig. 8b); PPO value MAE grows along the reasoning chain while VinePPO's shrinks (Fig. 8a) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Other claims: higher accuracy per unit KL budget (Fig. D.8) and more robust to higher sampling temperature (Fig. D.10) (Sec. 6.1) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Needs value-function learning: no. Simulator needs: reset to any intermediate state s_t of a sampled trajectory and run K full rollouts from it with the current policy; deterministic transitions assumed in the derivation; no privileged state — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)

#### Asymmetric actor-critic (Pinto et al. 2017, arXiv 1710.06542)
- Method (Sec. IV.A): off-policy DDPG where "the critic is trained on full states while the actor (or policy) gets rendered images as input"; critic input is "the concatenation of the current state s_t, the desired goal state g and the current action a_t"; critic loss L_c = (Q(s_t,a_t) − y_t)^2 with y_t = r_t + γQ(s_{t+1}, π(s_{t+1})); actor loss L_a = −E_s[Q(s, π(o))] with gradients flowing through the critic into the actor (Sec. III.B, IV.A) — [Pinto](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06542)
- Algorithm 1: each transition stores (s_t, o_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1}, o_{t+1}, g, g^o); the critic is trained on {s,a,r,s',g} and the actor on {o,a,r,o',g^o}; combined with HER for sparse rewards — [Pinto](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06542)
- Justification is purely practical: "Physics simulators give us access to both the full state ... and partial observations", "the full state is only used during training" (Sec. I). I found no analysis of estimator bias in the paper — [Pinto](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06542)
- Evidence (Sec. V.C, Fig. 5): asymmetric versions of both DDPG and HER "perform much better than their symmetric counterparts" on Particle, Reacher and Fetch Pick (5 seeds); a bottleneck auxiliary loss that predicts the full state from the actor's hidden layer further speeds training (Sec. V.E, Fig. 6). Real-robot transfer (Table I): asymmetric HER 5/5 on Pick, Forward Push, Block Move vs 0/5 for symmetric HER, 0/5 asymmetric DDPG, and failure without domain randomization — [Pinto](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06542)
- Needs value-function learning: yes (Q-critic on full state). Simulator needs: expose true state alongside the rendered observation for every transition; no intermediate resets — [Pinto](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06542)

#### Unbiased asymmetric actor-critic (Baisero & Amato 2021, arXiv 2105.11674)
- Policy-gradient theorem for POMDPs requires history values: ∇_θ L_policy = −E[Σ_t γ^t Q^π(h_t,a_t) ∇_θ log π(a_t;h_t)] (Eq. 4); A2C replaces Q(h,a) by TD error δ_t = R(s_t,a_t) + γV̂(h_{t+1}) − V̂(h_t) (Eq. 5–6) (Sec. 3) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Theorem 4.1: "In partially observable control problems, a time-invariant state value function V^π(s) is generally ill-defined", because V^π(s) = Σ_a Pr(a|s) Q^π(s,a) needs Pr(a|s) = Σ_h Pr(h|s) π(a;h) and Pr(h|s) has no well-defined random variables (histories of all lengths) (Sec. 4.1, Eqs. 9–11) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Theorem 4.2: "Even when well-defined, a time-invariant state value function V^π(s) is generally a biased estimate of V^π(h), i.e., it is not guaranteed that V^π(h) = E_{s|h}[V^π(s)]". Proof: two histories h' ≠ h'' with the same belief b(h') = b(h'') but different action distributions have V^π(h') ≠ V^π(h'') while E_{s|h'}[V(s)] = E_{s|h''}[V(s)] (Sec. 4.1) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Informal reason: "the state alone does not contain sufficient information to determine the agent's future behavior—which generally depends on the history", a form of "history aliasing"; particularly bad in tasks requiring information gathering, where "the state alone does not generally indicate whether the agent has collected the necessary information in the past or not" (Sec. 4) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Practical consequence: even when trained with unbiased Monte-Carlo returns (no bootstrapping), the state critic's "target will continue shifting indefinitely"; effects "vary depending on the amount of partial observability, on the agent's need to gather and remember information" (Sec. 4) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Theorem 4.3 (when state-only is fine): if there is a bijection φ: O → S between observations and states and the policy is reactive, V^π(s) is well-defined and unbiased; but "any POMDP which satisfies this assumption is really an MDP in disguise"; the authors cite Pinto et al.'s unoccluded-workspace images as an example of this near-bijective case, and note "if a control problem only deviates mildly from full observability, it is likely that a state critic will benefit the learning agent despite the theoretical issues" (Sec. 4.3) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Proposed fix (Sec. 5): history-state value V^π(h,s) = E[Σ_k γ^k R(s^(k),a^(k)) | h^(0), s^(0)] (Eq. 14), Bellman form V^π(h,s) = Σ_a π(a;h) Q^π(h,s,a), Q^π(h,s,a) = R(s,a) + γ E_{s',o|s,a}[V^π(hao, s')] (Eqs. 15–16). Theorem 5.1: "For arbitrary control problems and policies, V^π(h,s) is an unbiased estimate of V^π(h), i.e., V^π(h) = E_{s|h}[V^π(h,s)]" — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Theorem 5.2 (Asymmetric Policy Gradient): ∇_θ L_policy = −E[Σ_t γ^t Q^π(h_t,s_t,a_t) ∇_θ log π(a_t;h_t)] (Eq. 18); practical TD form δ_t = R(s_t,a_t) + γ V̂(h_{t+1},s_{t+1}) − V̂(h_t,s_t) (Eqs. 21–22). "the history h determines the future behavior of the agent, while the state s determines the future behavior of the environment" — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Variance of V(h,s) as an estimator of V(h) "depends on the history h via the belief-state Pr(S|h); in particular, low-uncertainty belief-states result in low variance, and deterministic belief-states result in no variance" (Sec. 5) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Interpretation (Sec. 5.1): the true state is "analytically irrelevant": "any other state sampled according to the belief-state s̃ ~ b(h), rather than the true system state, would also result in the same analytical bias/variance"; they conjecture the state works as a stochastic realization of a belief-state feature φ(h) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Evaluation (Sec. 6): on-policy A2C with full-episode sampling, 20 runs per configuration, 8 gridworld tasks (Heaven-Hell-3/4, Shopping-5/6, Car-Flag, Cleaner, Memory-Four-Rooms-7x7/9x9). State-only asymmetric A2C (A2C-asym-s) "either completely fails to perform the task (Heaven-Hell, Memory-Four-Rooms), or it slowly converges to a sub-optimal behavior (Shopping)", and in Cleaner "causing the performance to collapse after a certain point". History-state critic (A2C-asym-hs) matches the best of A2C/A2C-asym-s everywhere, beats both in Shopping, and "is the only method capable of learning to solve the task at all" in Heaven-Hell-3/4 — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Needs value-function learning: yes (critic V̂(h,s), which itself needs a history representation, e.g. an RNN). Simulator needs: true state s_t exposed for every step at training time; standard on-policy rollouts; no intermediate resets — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)

#### Recurrent model-free RL for POMDPs (Ni, Eysenbach, Salakhutdinov 2021, arXiv 2110.05038)
- Thesis: "the implementation of recurrent model-free RL matters"; design decisions "such as the actor-critic architecture, the underlying model-free RL algorithm, and context length in RNNs are especially important" (Sec. 1) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Their implementation is off-policy (TD3/SAC, SAC-Discrete) with recurrent actor and recurrent Q critic; recurrent PPO (PPO-GRU, Kostrikov 2018) and A2C-GRU appear only as prior baselines (Fig. 1, Table 2) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Finding 1, separate RNNs: "a shared encoder increases the gradient norm and hinders learning"; shared architecture "failed to learn either of these tasks"; "the gradient of critic loss may dominate the actor's" (Sec. 4, Sec. 5.2, Fig. 6) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Finding 2, backbone: off-policy TD3/SAC "generally works better than recurrent model-free RL implementations that use on-policy algorithms"; TD3 → SAC on Ant-P: 310.7 → 2123.5 (6.8×); exceptions Walker-V and Ant-Dir "where on-policy algorithms (PPO-GRU and RL2) outperform off-policy ones" (Sec. 5.2, Table 3) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Finding 3, context length: three settings short (5), medium (64), long (>100); "a medium length (64) dominates in all the best variants in most benchmarks"; but 64 → 400 on Cheetah-Vel +14%, and 64 → 5 gives 1.4× on Hopper-Generalize and 2.2× on Walker-V; "the choice of context length seems to be problem-specific and may require tuning" (Sec. 4, Sec. 5.2, Table 3) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Finding 4, inputs: adding past rewards to inputs (oa → oar) gives 981.6 → 1345.0 (1.3×) on Walker-P; "conditioning on other previous information, such as previous rewards, can increase return by up to 30%" (Sec. 4, Table 3) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- RNN cell: "no significant difference between LSTM and GRU" overall; GRU 1.2× better on Walker-Robust (Sec. 5.2) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Sequence handling (App. B): replay buffer stores whole trajectories; each sample is a (sub)trajectory of the context length, zero-padded if the episode is shorter; "We use the zero start state strategy (Hausknecht & Stone, 2015; Kapturowski et al., 2019) for simplicity, i.e. use zeros as the initial hidden state of RNNs" — i.e. no burn-in — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Scaling/efficiency: beats off-policy variBAD on Semi-Circle/Wind and on-policy variBAD on Cheetah-Dir but "fail to match their performance on Ant-Dir" (Fig. 2); beats MRPO on worst-case return but "is 17.5x slower than MRPO" (Sec. 5.1); 100× fewer samples than IMPALA+SR on Delayed-Catch/Key-to-Door with SAC-Discrete, LSTM and full-episode context (Sec. 5.1, Fig. 5) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Failure modes acknowledged: long-horizon/harder dynamics (Ant-Dir, Humanoid-Dir) not matched; oracle policies under-tuned; hyperparameters not tuned per task; wall-clock cost of RNN training (Sec. 5.1, 5.2, App. B) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Needs value-function learning: yes (recurrent Q critic). Simulator needs: ordinary episodic interaction; nothing privileged — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)

### Inferences
- For a FoW chess learner with a fixed opponent per iteration, the problem is a single-agent POMDP from the learner's perspective (opponent folded into the environment), so Baisero & Amato's theorems apply directly to the critic; none of the seven primary papers analyses the self-play/non-stationary two-player case, so game-theoretic convergence is out of scope of these sources.
- The exact +1/−1/0 terminal reward makes FoW chess structurally like the LLM-RL settings in GRPO/RLOO/VinePPO (single terminal scalar, long trajectory) and unlike MuJoCo/Atari, so the λ = 1 (no bootstrapping) findings in RLOO and VinePPO are the most relevant variance/bias evidence, with the caveat that both papers attribute their tolerance of high variance to strong pre-trained initialization, which an RL-from-scratch chess policy does not have.

### Gaps
- Pinto et al. do not discuss estimator bias at all; the bias analysis exists only in Baisero & Amato, whose experiments are small gridworlds with on-policy A2C and 20 seeds; no evidence from either paper on large-scale games.
- Ni et al. did not test their design choices on PPO (their method is off-policy); the recurrent-PPO-specific evidence has to come from the 37-details blog (below), which is an implementation description without ablations of the LSTM details.

## Key question 1: When is a critic conditioned on the true state unbiased under partial observability, and what must the asymmetric baseline condition on?

### Takeaway
Baisero & Amato prove that a state-only critic V(s) is generally ill-defined and, even when well-defined, biased as an estimator of the history value V(h) required by the POMDP policy-gradient theorem; the fix is a history-state critic V(h,s) (or Q(h,s,a)), which is unbiased for arbitrary policies and environments because V(h) = E_{s~b(h)}[V(h,s)]. A state-only critic is unbiased only when observations and states are in bijection and the policy is reactive (an "MDP in disguise").

### Cited Findings
- The POMDP policy-gradient theorem requires history values Q^π(h_t,a_t); "replacing them with other state-based values will generally result in biased gradients and a general loss of theoretical guarantees" (Sec. 4, Eq. 4) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Theorem 4.1: time-invariant V^π(s) is generally ill-defined (Pr(h|s) has no valid random variables) (Sec. 4.1) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Theorem 4.2: even when well-defined (reactive policy + observation function depending only on current state, Sec. 4.2), V^π(s) is biased: two histories with equal beliefs but different action distributions give equal E_{s|h}[V(s)] but different V(h) (Sec. 4.1–4.2) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Theorem 4.3: unbiased only under a bijection φ: O → S plus reactive policy; "this assumption is intrinsically incompatible with partial observability"; mild deviations from full observability may still benefit from a state critic (Sec. 4.3) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Theorem 5.1: V^π(h,s) is an unbiased estimate of V^π(h) "for arbitrary control problems and policies"; Theorem 5.2: the policy gradient can use Q^π(h_t,s_t,a_t) in place of Q^π(h_t,a_t) (Sec. 5, Eqs. 14–20) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- The critic must receive the history h as input "so it can still predict reasonable estimates of the agent's expected future discounted returns" and the state s to "exploit state information while introducing no bias" (Sec. 5) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Variance of V(h,s) around V(h) is governed by belief uncertainty; deterministic beliefs give zero variance (Sec. 5) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Empirically, the state-only critic fails or collapses on information-gathering tasks (Heaven-Hell, Memory-Four-Rooms, Cleaner) while the history-state critic is the only method that solves Heaven-Hell (Sec. 6.1.1, Fig. 3) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)
- Pinto et al.'s critic conditions on full state s_t (plus goal g and action a_t) only, not on the observation or history; their observations are unoccluded camera images (Sec. IV.A) — [Pinto](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06542); Baisero & Amato classify this as the near-bijective special case (Sec. 4.3) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)

### Inferences
- In FoW chess the true state (full board plus side to move) does not encode what the agent has sensed; two histories with the same full board can have very different beliefs and future behaviour, which is exactly the Theorem 4.2 construction. A critic V(full board) is therefore a biased baseline for the recurrent actor, and the bias is largest precisely where sensing/information-gathering decisions matter.
- The unbiased construction for FoW chess is V̂(h_t, s_t): a critic that takes the actor's observation history (e.g. its own RNN state or a separate critic RNN over the same observation stream, cf. Ni et al. on separate RNNs) concatenated with the full board. Baisero & Amato's "state as feature" interpretation also implies the critic could receive any sample from b(h) (e.g. a sampled consistent hidden board) with the same bias/variance, which is relevant if the simulator state includes opponent-private information one wishes to abstract.
- Because V(h,s) as an estimator of V(h) has zero variance when the belief is deterministic, the asymmetric critic's advantage over a symmetric critic should be greatest in early/uncertain game phases and vanish as the position becomes fully determined (e.g. after captures reveal the board).
- Baisero & Amato's analysis covers a fixed policy; with self-play the "environment" (opponent) changes between iterations, so the critic must be retrained on-policy each iteration as in PPO/A2C anyway.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies the magnitude of state-only bias on a large game, nor whether an RNN-conditioned critic scales; Baisero & Amato's tasks are gridworlds with on-policy A2C.
- Neither paper treats multi-agent partial observability (where part of the true state is another agent's private information); the theorems are stated for single-agent POMDPs.

## Key question 2: What do GRPO / RLOO / VinePPO require of the environment, and how does that transfer to a sequential game with hidden information and a recurrent history?

### Takeaway
GRPO and RLOO require only K i.i.d. trajectories from the same initial state (a "prompt") and score the whole trajectory with one terminal reward; VinePPO additionally requires resetting the simulator to arbitrary intermediate states to run K rollouts per step. Transferring to FoW chess, the group methods degenerate to trajectory-level REINFORCE with a win-rate baseline from the same start position, while VinePPO-style rollouts from the true simulator state s_t with the actor's history h_t are, by Baisero & Amato's Theorem 5.1, Monte-Carlo estimates of the unbiased history-state value V(h_t,s_t) without learning a critic.

### Cited Findings
- GRPO: "for each question q, GRPO samples a group of outputs {o_1..o_G} from the old policy" and uses the group-normalized reward (r_i − mean)/std as the advantage of every token in o_i (Sec. 4.1.1–4.1.2); DeepSeekMath used G = 64 outputs per question and a single policy update per sampling stage (Sec. 4.2) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- GRPO's gradient coefficient is Â_{i,t} + β(π_ref/π_θ − 1) (Eq. 21), i.e. no per-step credit assignment beyond the shared group advantage (App. A.1.6) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- RLOO: k i.i.d. samples y^(1..k) ~ π_θ(·|x) per prompt; baseline for sample i is the mean reward of the other k−1 samples; needs the "entire generation as a single action" bandit view, justified by deterministic transitions (Sec. 2.3, 3.3) — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- RLOO reports k = 2 and k = 4 only; the extra samples cost "increased sampling time during training" (Sec. 2.3, Table 1) — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- RLOO argues the no-bootstrap, no-critic regime is safe because of "the extremely strong initialization of the policy (a pre-trained LLM)" and notes prior evidence that REINFORCE "fails in the presence of large action spaces" came from "random or weak initialization" (Sec. 3.1) — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- VinePPO: needs the "resetting property" to re-feed the partial context for s_t and sample K rollouts; the Vine idea is "limited to environments that allow intermediate state resets", which language, Go and Chess provide; footnote: derivation assumes a deterministic environment (Sec. 2, 4) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- VinePPO's MC advantage is unbiased for any K ≥ 1; K trades variance against sampling cost; auxiliary rollouts are not used for gradient updates (Sec. 4) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- VinePPO reports that a well-trained PPO value network (explained variance 0.7–0.9) still ranks candidate next steps near chance and misclassifies bad states as good, and that this is the only difference between PPO and VinePPO in their runs (Sec. 6.1, 7) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- VinePPO in Appendix C.5 characterizes GRPO/RLOO as computing a baseline that is "MC estimate of value but solely for the initial state" (Fig. 2 caption) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Baisero & Amato: V(h) = E_{s|h}[V(h,s)], so a return sampled from the true state while the agent continues from its history is an unbiased MC sample of the history value (Theorem 5.1, Sec. 5) — [Baisero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.11674)

### Inferences
- Trajectory-level GRPO/RLOO in FoW chess: every game starts from the same position, so the "prompt" is the start state and K games against the same opponent give a leave-one-out win-rate baseline. With rewards in {−1,0,+1}, GRPO's std-normalization is undefined when all K outcomes tie (std = 0), and the group advantage is a constant sign applied to every move of an ~80-ply game, i.e. REINFORCE with no temporal credit assignment; RLOO's justification (strong initialization keeps gradients small) does not hold for a from-scratch policy, so I would expect much higher variance than in the LLM setting. A hybrid where the "prompt" is a sampled opening position (K games from the same opening) is a natural adaptation but is not evaluated by any of these papers.
- Hidden information breaks the bandit reduction: RLOO's deterministic-transition argument does not hold because the opponent (and sensing results) are stochastic; the methods remain unbiased (REINFORCE with a baseline is unbiased under stochastic environments), but the baseline is now an estimate of the start-state value against the current opponent only.
- VinePPO transfer: resetting to an intermediate step requires restoring (i) the full simulator state s_t (both sides' pieces), (ii) the actor's recurrent state/history h_t, and (iii) the opponent's internal state if it is also recurrent. Given these, K rollouts estimate V(h_t, s_t) — by Baisero & Amato Theorem 5.1 an unbiased MC estimate of the history value, so VinePPO-from-true-state yields exactly the unbiased asymmetric baseline without a learned critic. Cost scales as K rollouts per training step, each a full game, and because opponent moves are stochastic the per-rollout variance is higher than in the deterministic LLM case; VinePPO's own figures show per-iteration cost 2–5× PPO even in the deterministic case.
- A learned V̂(h,s) critic (Baisero & Amato) and VinePPO rollouts estimate the same quantity; the former amortizes with function approximation (and inherits the value-network inaccuracy VinePPO documents), the latter pays simulator time instead. The simulator's "expose true state + reset to state" capabilities are precisely what makes the choice available.

### Gaps
- No paper applies GRPO/RLOO/VinePPO to a multi-step stochastic game or to a recurrent policy; all evidence is from autoregressive LLM generation with deterministic transitions and pre-trained initialization.
- No source reports how to handle the std = 0 case in GRPO's normalization or what K is needed when rewards are ternary; I found no numbers on K vs variance beyond VinePPO's K ∈ {1,3,9} accuracy ablation.

## Key question 3: What does Ni et al. report about recurrent RL on POMDPs — which design choices mattered?

### Takeaway
Ni et al. find that (1) separate actor and critic RNNs are consistently better (shared encoders blow up gradient norms and can fail outright), (2) a strong off-policy backbone (TD3/SAC) usually beats on-policy recurrent PPO/A2C, (3) context length matters a lot but is task-specific (64 was the best default; both 5 and 400 won somewhere), and (4) inputs must contain enough information (observations, actions, rewards) to infer the hidden state. They use zero initial hidden states, zero-padded sub-trajectories of the context length, and no burn-in.

### Cited Findings
- Summary findings 1–4 (Sec. 5.2): separate recurrent actor/critic weights "can boost performance, likely because it avoids gradient explosion"; off-policy backbones improve asymptotic performance and sample efficiency "in most environments"; context length "has a large influence on task performance, but the optimal length seems to be task-specific. Starting with a medium length is a good strategy"; inputs must "contain enough information to infer the POMDP hidden states" — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Shared encoder "failed to learn either of these tasks"; gradient-norm plot shows critic loss dominating (Fig. 6, App. E.3) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Table 3 effect sizes: oa → oar 1.3× (Walker-P); TD3 → SAC 6.8× (Ant-P); LSTM → GRU 1.2× (Walker-Robust); Len 64 → 400 +14% (Cheetah-Vel); Len 64 → 5 1.4× (Hopper-Generalize) and 2.2× (Walker-V) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Prior work used context lengths from 1 to 2048 (Table 2); Ni et al. test 5 / 64 / >100 and use full episode length for the temporal-credit-assignment tasks (Sec. 4, 5.1) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Recurrent PPO-GRU (Kostrikov 2018) and A2C-GRU are the on-policy baselines that their best off-policy variant beats on occluded locomotion (Fig. 1); on Walker-V and Ant-Dir on-policy methods (PPO-GRU, RL2) win (Sec. 5.2) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Sequence sampling: replay stores trajectories; sampled sub-trajectories of the context length, zero-padded; "zero start state strategy" for RNN initial hidden states (App. B) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Architecture: separate one-layer LSTM/GRU per actor and critic, MLP embedders for observation / previous action / reward, and a shortcut from the current-observation embedding to the MLP head "may reduce the burden of accurately memorizing it in RNN" (App. B, Fig. 7) — [Ni](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05038)
- Recurrent-PPO implementation details (not from Ni): LSTM states initialized to zero and reset at episode boundaries; mini-batches keep sequence order per sub-environment rather than shuffling; initial LSTM state saved before rollout and hidden states reconstructed sequentially during the update "to match rollout probability distributions" (LSTM details 2–5) — [37 details](https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/)

### Inferences
- For FoW chess the relevant context is the whole game (sensing results early in the game stay informative), which corresponds to Ni et al.'s "full episode length" setting used in the temporal-credit-assignment tasks; their finding that shorter contexts sometimes win was on locomotion tasks where hidden variables (velocity) are locally recoverable, which does not describe FoW chess.
- Ni et al.'s separate-RNN finding and Baisero & Amato's requirement that the critic see h both point to a critic with its own recurrent encoder over the observation stream plus the true state, rather than reusing the actor's hidden state (which would couple critic gradients into the actor).
- Their "inputs must suffice to infer hidden state" finding translates to feeding the actor/critic the sense-square results and own moves explicitly, not just board views; the terminal-only reward carries no per-step information so the "previous reward" input is uninformative here.
- The on-policy/off-policy comparison is only indirectly relevant: Ni et al. show recurrent PPO can be beaten by recurrent SAC/TD3 on locomotion, but their exceptions and the lack of a tuned recurrent-PPO implementation mean the result should not be read as "recurrent PPO is weak".

### Gaps
- Ni et al. do not ablate burn-in or truncated BPTT (they use zero start states throughout); the 37-details blog describes but does not ablate the LSTM handling for PPO. I found no controlled evidence on burn-in vs zero-state for recurrent PPO in these sources.
- No experiments with discrete, large action spaces plus long full-game contexts comparable to chess.

## Key question 4: Which PPO hyperparameters and tricks are evidenced as important, and which are folklore?

### Takeaway
Evidence from Schulman et al., Engstrom et al., Andrychowicz et al. (as relayed by the 37-details blog) and RLOO: clipping (or some step-size control) matters versus a plain ratio objective in MuJoCo, but code-level details (reward scaling, LR annealing, orthogonal init, observation normalization) matter as much or more; value-loss clipping has no supporting evidence and may hurt; per-minibatch advantage normalization has little effect; the entropy bonus has no evidence in continuous control; separate policy/value networks help; KL early stopping is a Spinning Up heuristic not present in the original PPO; in the LLM/terminal-reward regime clipping fires <5% of the time and removing it does not hurt.

### Cited Findings
- Clipping vs none vs KL: avg normalized score −0.39 without clipping/penalty, 0.82 with ε = 0.2, 0.68–0.74 with adaptive KL, 0.62–0.72 with fixed KL (Table 1, Sec. 6.1) — [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)
- Nine code-level optimizations identified in the baselines PPO2 implementation: value-function clipping, reward scaling by std of a rolling discounted return, orthogonal init with layer scaling, Adam LR annealing, reward clipping, observation normalization, observation clipping, tanh activations, global gradient-norm clipping at 0.5 (Sec. 3) — [Engstrom](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12729)
- Full ablation of the first four shows "many code-level optimizations are necessary for PPO to attain its claimed performance" (Sec. 3, Fig. 1) — [Engstrom](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12729)
- Table 2 (MuJoCo, ≥80 agents each, 95% CIs): PPO 3292/2513/806; PPO-M (no optimizations) 2735/2142/674; TRPO 2791/2043/586; TRPO+ (with optimizations) 3050/2466/1030 on Walker2d/Hopper/Humanoid; "code-level optimizations contribute to algorithms' increased performance often significantly more than the choice of algorithm" (Sec. 5) — [Engstrom](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12729)
- Table 3: PPO-NoClip (with optimizations) 2867/2371/831 vs PPO 3292/2513/806 vs PPO-M 2735/2142/674; "the clipping mechanism is not necessary to achieve high performance", "PPO-NoClip performs uniformly better than PPO-M" (Sec. 5) — [Engstrom](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12729)
- Trust region: the first PPO step is identical to an unclipped step (ratios start at 1), so the enforced region depends on the optimizer not the objective; "all three algorithms fail to maintain a ratio-based trust region"; both PPO and PPO-M keep mean KL bounded but with different shapes (PPO-M KL trends up; PPO KL peaks mid-training) (Sec. 4, Fig. 2) — [Engstrom](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12729)
- Value-loss clipping: Engstrom et al. found "no evidence" it helps; Andrychowicz et al. (decision C13, Fig. 43) found it hurts performance — [37 details](https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/)
- Entropy bonus: Andrychowicz et al. found "no evidence" the entropy term improves continuous-control performance (decision C13, Figs. 76–77) — [37 details](https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/)
- Per-minibatch advantage normalization: Andrychowicz et al. found it "doesn't significantly affect performance" (decision C67, Fig. 35) — [37 details](https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/); search-summary of the paper states the same ("per-minibatch advantage normalization seems not to affect the performance too much") — [Andrychowicz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05990)
- Observation normalization "very helpful" (C64, Fig. 33); observation clipping after normalization did not help (C65); separate policy/value networks better than shared on MuJoCo (C47, Fig. 15); global gradient clipping gives a small boost (C68, Fig. 34); GAE beat N-step returns (C6); LR annealing small gains (C31); PPO clipped objective beat vanilla PG, V-trace, AWR, V-MPO in most tasks — [37 details](https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/)
- Andrychowicz et al. studied >50 choices with >250,000 agents on five continuous-control tasks; reported: input normalization crucial on all envs but Hopper; value-function normalization "influences performance very strongly" but helps on some tasks and "significantly hurts" on Walker2d; gradient clipping small boost — [Andrychowicz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05990) (summary via search result; full-text recommendations not read directly)
- Adam ε = 1e-5 (not the 1e-8 default) and orthogonal init (√2 hidden, 0.01 policy head, 1 value head) are needed for faithful reproduction; Atari pixel scaling to [0,1] is "critical; without it, first policy update causes KL divergence explosion" — [37 details](https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/)
- KL early stopping is listed among "auxiliary details not in official PPO" ("used in spinningup, not baselines"), alongside clip-range annealing and invalid-action masking ("critical for Gym-μRTS", used by AlphaStar/OpenAI Five) — [37 details](https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/)
- Spinning Up PPO-Clip: g(ε,A) form of the objective; "early stopping based on approximate KL"; defaults clip_ratio 0.2, pi_lr 3e-4, vf_lr 1e-3, 80 policy and 80 value gradient steps per epoch, λ = 0.97, target_kl 0.01 — [Spinning Up](https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/algorithms/ppo.html)
- Adaptive KL penalty rule in the original paper: β halved/doubled when KL falls below d_targ/1.5 or exceeds 1.5·d_targ; "the algorithm is not very sensitive to" the 1.5 and 2 constants (Sec. 4) — [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)
- Terminal-reward regime (RLHF): clipping active "< 5% of the time per batch"; removing policy and value clipping "does not impact learning meaningfully"; λ = 1 best, reward decreases monotonically as λ decreases (Sec. 3.1–3.2, Fig. 1) — [RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- VinePPO's tuned LLM-PPO: λ = 1, γ = 1, ε = 0.2, value clip 0.2, 2 inner epochs, constant KL coefficient (not adaptive), dropout disabled, rewards not normalized (already in [0,1]), advantage normalized across batch and ranks (App. C.2, Table 1) — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- GRPO: KL added to the loss via the positive k3 estimator, coefficient 0.04, single update per batch (Sec. 4.1.1, 4.2) — [GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)

### Inferences
- Evidence-backed for a FoW-chess PPO: some step-size control (clip or KL penalty) when multiple epochs are run; separate actor/critic networks (Andrychowicz C47 plus Ni et al.'s separate-RNN finding); orthogonal init and Adam ε; global grad-norm clipping; observation normalization is moot for binary board planes. Likely folklore or harmful: value-loss clipping, entropy bonus (unless exploration collapse is observed; no positive evidence in these sources), per-minibatch advantage normalization (neutral), observation clipping.
- With a ±1/0 terminal reward, reward scaling/normalization (Engstrom's most impactful trick) is unnecessary (VinePPO likewise skips it for [0,1] rewards), and λ close to 1 is supported by both RLOO and VinePPO for terminal-only rewards; however, γ = λ = 1 on ~80-ply games with a from-scratch policy will have far higher variance than in the LLM setting, so λ < 1 with an unbiased V(h,s) critic (Baisero & Amato) is the principled way to trade variance for bias here.
- KL early stopping is an implementation heuristic without a controlled ablation in these sources; Engstrom's result that mean KL stays bounded even for PPO-M suggests it is a safety net rather than a driver of performance.

### Gaps
- I did not read Andrychowicz et al.'s full text; its findings are cited via the 37-details blog and a search summary, and it covers continuous control only. No comparable large-scale study exists for discrete-action, recurrent, or two-player settings.
- No source ablates entropy bonus or advantage normalization in a sparse-terminal-reward discrete game; the "folklore" verdicts above are extrapolated from continuous control and LLM settings.
