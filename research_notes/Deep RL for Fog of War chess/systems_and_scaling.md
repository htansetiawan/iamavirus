# Systems side of end-to-end deep RL for partially observed board games: throughput, batching, league infrastructure, compute budgets

Scope: sizing a Fog-of-War (FoW) chess bot trained on one RTX PRO 5000 (48 GB) with a Python simulator currently at ~800 learner decisions/s (~2.9M decisions/hour), deployed on 2 CPU cores via ONNX Runtime with a 4 s/move budget.

Method note: full texts of the AlphaZero, DeepNash, KataGo, IMPALA, EnvPool, Sample Factory, PufferLib, OpenAI Five, Brax, TStarBot-X, Podracer and both RBC tournament papers were extracted from the arXiv/PMLR PDFs with pdftotext and quoted directly. The R2D2 PDF on OpenReview was behind a CAPTCHA, so R2D2 facts are cited to secondary sources that quote the paper (DI-engine docs and a UT Austin lecture deck); treat those as second-hand. The AlphaStar Nature paper is paywalled; its compute figures are cited via TStarBot-X's comparison table.

---

## Key question 1: How many learner decisions / how much wall-clock did published game-RL systems need, and how did strength scale with samples?

### Takeaway
Search-based self-play systems (AlphaZero, KataGo) reached superhuman chess/Go strength on 10^8 to 10^9 *search-backed* training positions, whereas model-free actor-learner systems (DeepNash, OpenAI Five, AlphaStar) consumed 10^10 to 10^12 raw environment steps on hundreds to thousands of accelerators. KataGo is the best efficiency anchor: 241M training samples / 4.2M games in 19 days on ~27 V100s, with the 6-block net reaching "strong/top amateur" after ~0.75 days. At 2.9M decisions/hour, the FoW pipeline would match KataGo's *sample* count in ~3.5 days, but each KataGo sample carries a 100-600-playout MCTS target; no published RBC/FoW bot has been trained end-to-end by self-play RL, and the competition winners all lean on Stockfish/Lc0 plus belief tracking.

### Cited Findings

**AlphaZero (chess, shogi, Go)**
- "Training proceeded for 700,000 steps (mini-batches of size 4,096) starting from randomly initialised parameters, using 5,000 first-generation TPUs (15) to generate self-play games and 64 second-generation TPUs to train the neural networks." — [Silver et al. 2017, arXiv 1712.01815](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815)
- "In chess, AlphaZero outperformed Stockfish after just 4 hours (300k steps); in shogi, AlphaZero outperformed Elmo after less than 2 hours (110k steps); and in Go, AlphaZero outperformed AlphaGo Lee (29) after 8 hours (165k steps)." — [arXiv 1712.01815](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815)
- Table S3: Chess — 700k mini-batches, 9 h training time, 44 million training games, 800 sims/move at 40 ms thinking time; Shogi — 12 h, 24 million games; Go — 34 h, 21 million games. — [arXiv 1712.01815, Table S3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815)
- "During training, each MCTS used 800 simulations." Evaluation: "AlphaZero searches just 80 thousand positions per second in chess ... compared to 70 million for Stockfish", with "a single machine with 4 TPUs" per MCTS. — [arXiv 1712.01815](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815)
- Footnote: "AlphaGo Master and AlphaGo Zero were ultimately trained for 100 times this length of time; we do not reproduce that effort here." — [arXiv 1712.01815](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815)
- KataGo's accounting of the same run: "DeepMind's main reported run for Go using 5000 TPUs for several days, totaling about 41 TPU-years"; "ELF OpenGo ... used 2000 V100 GPUs for about 13-14 days, or about 74 GPU-years". — [Wu 2019, arXiv 1902.10565](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)

**KataGo (Go; the efficiency anchor)**
- "It surpasses the strength of ELF OpenGo after training on about 27 V100 GPUs for 19 days, a total of about 1.4 GPU-years, or about a factor of 50 reduction." — [arXiv 1902.10565](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)
- "In total, KataGo's main run lasted for 19 days using a maximum of 28 V100 GPUs at any time (averaging 26-27) and generated about 241 million training samples across 4.2 million games." — [arXiv 1902.10565](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)
- Net growth schedule: "(b, c) began at (6, 96) and switched to (10, 128), (15, 192), and (20, 256), at roughly 0.75 days, 1.75 days, and 7.5 days, respectively." Table 7 strength per size at 1600 visits: b6x96 "Strong/Top Amateur" (-1276 Elo vs ELF), b10x128 "Strong Professional" (-850), b15x192 "Superhuman" (-329), b20x256 "Superhuman" (+76). — [arXiv 1902.10565, Sec. 2 and Table 7](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)
- Playout cap randomization: "(N, n) = (600, 100) initially, annealing up to (1000, 200) after the first two days of training" — i.e. most moves get only 100-200 playouts, a minority get 600-1000. — [arXiv 1902.10565, Sec. 3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)
- Training: "batch size of 256 (the largest size fitting on one GPU) ... fixed per-sample learning rate of 6e-5"; data window "beginning at 250,000 samples and increasing to about 22 million by the end of the main run"; gating "winning at least 100 out of 200 test games". — [arXiv 1902.10565](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)
- Ablation Table 2 (factor increase in training time to reach the 2-day Elo when a technique is removed): Playout Cap Randomization 1.37x, Forced Playouts + Policy Target Pruning 1.25x, Global Pooling 1.60x, Auxiliary Policy Targets 1.30x, Aux Ownership and Score Targets 1.65x, Game-specific Features 1.55x; "The product of the acceleration factors shown is approximately 9.1x." — [arXiv 1902.10565, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)
- Footnote 2: "Using our code, it is possible to reach strong or top human amateur strength starting from nothing on even single GPUs in mere days, and several people have in fact already done so." — [arXiv 1902.10565](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)
- Later runs: g104 "halted after 19 days" with "max of 28 V100 GPUs"; g170 "lasted from December 2019 to June 2020 for about 5 months", "used about 46 GPUs for most of its duration. Of these, 40 were for self-play data generation", "took only 12-14 days to surpass its earlier 19-day 'g104' run", and ended "more than 700 Elo stronger". — [KataGo TrainingHistory.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/TrainingHistory.md)

**DeepNash (Stratego; the closest published analogue: imperfect information, model-free, 10x10 board, long episodes)**
- Hardware: "To train the final agent we used 768 TPU nodes used for Learners and 256 TPU nodes for Actors." — [Perolat et al. 2022, arXiv 2206.15378, Supp. "Infrastructure and Setup"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Table 2 training parameters: "max number of steps 7.21M steps"; trajectory "tmax 3600"; "Batch size (number of trajectories per step) 768"; learning rate 0.00005; v-trace rho-bar = c-bar = 1.0; regularisation schedule Delta_m = 10k steps for m <= 100, 100k for 100 < m <= 165, 35k for m > 165. — [arXiv 2206.15378, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Learner mechanics: "The IMPALA architecture (44) is adapted to the needs of the R-NaD algorithm, namely storing full episodes in the replay buffer. The learner reads a batch of full episodes, splits them into the sequence of ordered chunks of fixed length (mini batches), and computes the parameter updates on that sequence in a reverse order." — [arXiv 2206.15378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Pipeline: "The DeepNash training pipeline follows Sebulba Podracer architecture"; "The Actors environment-agent loop is implemented in C++ using fibers and the native open spiel (80) interfaces. This provides better overall throughput for playing games."; "Actors run environment and inference and use CPU and a small amount of accelerator FLOPs." — [arXiv 2206.15378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Observation: "the observation contains 82 stacked frames encoded in a single tensor" (own pieces, public info about both sides, encoding of the last 40 moves); network is "a U-Net torso with residual blocks and skip-connections ... and four heads"; action space "3600 possible actions at every step". — [arXiv 2206.15378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Result: "a win rate of over 97%" vs bots; "ranks among the top 3 players, both on the annual (2022) and all-times leaderboards, with a win rate of 84%" on Gravon. — [arXiv 2206.15378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)

**OpenAI Five (Dota 2; LSTM policy, PPO, asynchronous rollouts)**
- "After ten months of training using 770+-50 PFlops/s-days of compute, it defeated the Dota 2 world champions"; "we used batch sizes of 1 to 3 million timesteps (grouped in unrolled LSTM windows of length 16) ... a model with over 150 million parameters ... OpenAI Five trained for 180 days". — [Berner et al. 2019, arXiv 1912.06680, Sec. 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)
- Appendix table: OpenAI Five used 480-1,536 optimizer GPUs, 500-1,440 rollout GPUs, 80,000-172,800 rollout CPUs, total batch 983,040-3,145,728 timesteps, LSTM unroll 16, sample reuse 0.8-2.7; "Rerun" used 512 optimizer GPUs, 512 rollout GPUs, 51,200 rollout CPUs, batch 983,040 timesteps. — [arXiv 1912.06680, Appendix C table](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)
- "Rerun took 2 months and 150 +- 5 PFlops/s-days of compute"; "Using the scale of Rerun, we were able to reach superhuman performance in two months." Batch scaling: "Rerun's batch size (983k time steps) had a speedup factor of around 2.5x over the baseline batch size (123k)"; "Our results suggest that speedup is less than linear." — [arXiv 1912.06680, Sec. 4.2-4.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)
- Data freshness: "Rollout machines produce 7.5 steps per second; they send data every 256 steps, or 34 seconds of game play"; "Our final system targets sample reuse ~1 in all our experiments." — [arXiv 1912.06680](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)

**AlphaStar (StarCraft II) and an academic reproduction (TStarBot-X)**

- DeepMind blog: AlphaStar "was ranked above 99.8% of active players on Battle.net"; supervised initial policy played "better than 84% of active players" before RL. — [DeepMind AlphaStar blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphastar-grandmaster-level-in-starcraft-ii-using-multi-agent-reinforcement-learning)

**Reconnaissance Blind Chess (RBC) learning efforts — the direct FoW analogue**
- 2022 NeurIPS tournament: "18 bots played each other in 9,180 games"; time control "15 minutes per game plus 5 seconds per turn (sense and move), per player"; "a practical, sound (unexploitable) algorithm remains unknown." — [Gardner et al. 2023, PMLR 220](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a.html)
- 2022 final ranking (Bayesian Elo): StrangeFish2 1762 (1), Fianchetto 1644 (2), Kevin 1623 (3), Chateaux 1621 (4), ROOKie 1551 (5), Oracle 1465 (6), ... uccchess 1025 (14). — [PMLR 220, Table 3](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a.html)
- StrangeFish2 (winner): "tracks all possible locations of opponent pieces ... board state value heuristics – which continue to augment Stockfish engine analysis". Fianchetto: "uses Leela Chess Zero (Lc0) to model a probability distribution over opponent board states as a POMDP". — [PMLR 220, Table 1](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a.html)
- Kevin (3rd, 2022): "Predicts opponent actions (both senses and moves) using a neural network. The neural network is trained with imitation learning on game logs of StrangeFish2 ... pass through a residual neural network (ResNet) and a long short-term memory (LSTM) network, which output a distribution over legal actions ... using Stockfish to evaluate board states." — [PMLR 220, Table 1](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a.html)
- Chateaux / penumbra (4th 2022, 3rd 2021, won 2020): "The network was trained on approximately 600,000 games available online ... plans with playouts according to a variant of Monte Carlo tree search guided by the neural network." — [PMLR 220, Table 1](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a.html)
- Penumbra details: "Training and evaluation were run on four RTX 2080 Ti GPUs"; residual network with 10 residual blocks, input "8x8x104" synopsis bitboards, 14 headsets of 5 heads; training set 85.6k games (16M-17.6M training actions); "The program processes approximately 4,000 nodes per second" with batch size 256 and "nparticles = 4096"; won the "official 2020 reconnaissance blind chess competition versus 33 other programs". — [Clark 2021, arXiv 2110.01810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810); summary of data/compute per [ar5iv rendering](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.01810)
- Conflict on how penumbra learned: the 2021 tournament paper says "penumbra, which learned state values from reinforcement learning on RBC games rather than relying on an engine" — [Perrotta et al. 2022, PMLR 176](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a.html); but Clark 2021 describes supervised training on downloaded historical games plus 5,000 locally generated games, with no self-play RL — [arXiv 2110.01810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810). Treat Clark 2021 (primary) as authoritative.
- uccchess (14th, 2022): the only bot described as using RL, and only for opponent classification: "a set of 20 neural networks to replace the chess engine ... The opponent's class is selected by an agent that is trained using reinforcement learning. During its training, uccchess plays againts several predefined bots". — [PMLR 220, Table 1](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a.html)
- 2021 tournament: "After 30 rounds and 9,180 games, the bot Fianchetto won convincingly, with a 91% overall" win rate; Elo: Fianchetto 1759, StrangeFish2 1662, penumbra 1584, Kevin 1544, Oracle 1503. — [PMLR 176](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a.html)
- 2021 paper notes the structural barrier for learned methods: training whole-game deep-CFR/NFSP nets "In large, complex games, training the necessary neural networks could" be prohibitive (section 3, related work). — [PMLR 176](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v176/perrotta22a.html)

### Inferences
- Sample budget arithmetic for the FoW pipeline: 800 decisions/s = 2.88M/h = 69M/day = 2.1B/month. KataGo's 241M samples would be matched in ~3.5 days by count alone, and AlphaZero's 700k x 4096 = 2.87B sampled positions in ~6 weeks. But every KataGo/AlphaZero sample is a *search-improved* policy target (100-1000 playouts), so one of their samples is worth far more than one model-free PPO decision; the model-free systems (DeepNash, OpenAI Five, AlphaStar) are the right reference class and they each consumed on the order of 10^10-10^12 environment steps.
- DeepNash's step count is ambiguous in the paper: "7.21M steps" with a batch of 768 trajectories. If a step is one batch of full episodes, that is ~5.5B trajectories (times hundreds of moves each); if a step is one fixed-length chunk mini-batch (as the "splits them into ... chunks" text suggests), total episodes are lower by the chunks-per-episode factor. Either reading places DeepNash at >= 10^10 learner decisions on ~1,000 TPU nodes, i.e. 3-4 orders of magnitude above what 2.9M/h delivers in a month (reasoning, not a quoted figure).
- Transfer caveat: Stratego has a 3600-way action space, 10x10 board and 82-plane observations; FoW chess has a smaller branching factor and bitboard-cheap rules, but similar information-set explosion. DeepNash's R-NaD regularised-Nash training is what made model-free self-play converge; plain PPO self-play without such regularisation is not what any of these systems used in imperfect-information settings.
- The realistic strength target implied by the RBC literature: all top RBC bots combine belief tracking with an engine evaluator (Stockfish/Lc0/Maia); the best purely learned evaluator (penumbra) placed 3rd-4th and was trained supervised on <1M human games with 4 GPUs, achieving ~4,000 NN-guided nodes/s at play time. A single-GPU end-to-end RL agent should be benchmarked against that tier, not DeepNash.
- OpenAI Five's finding that batch-size speedup is sublinear (2.5x from 8x batch) and that sample reuse >1 and staleness hurt suggests that for a single-GPU pipeline the lever is wall-clock samples/s, not larger batches.

### Gaps
- No published RBC or FoW-chess agent trained end-to-end by self-play deep RL with reported sample counts was found; the RBC literature contains supervised/imitation networks (penumbra, Kevin) and one RL-for-opponent-classification bot (uccchess). The "learner decisions to strong play" question therefore has no direct RBC datapoint.
- DeepNash's exact total environment steps, wall-clock, and actor count per TPU node are not stated; the paper gives TPU node counts and learner-step counts only.
- AlphaStar's per-agent step counts and "years of experience" figure could not be verified from the Nature paper (paywalled); only the compute table reproduced in TStarBot-X was accessible.
- KataGo's Elo-vs-samples curve (Figure 4) could not be read numerically from the text extraction; only the milestone table and ablation factors are quoted.

## Key question 2: Which engineering pattern gives order-of-magnitude simulator speedups, and what throughput do EnvPool / Sample Factory / PufferLib / IMPALA report?

### Takeaway
Python-process vectorisation (gym Subprocess) scales almost flat; a C++ thread-pool with batched, asynchronous stepping (EnvPool) gives 2.8x on a 12-core laptop and 15-19x on 256 cores, and Sample Factory-style fully asynchronous actor/policy-worker/learner decomposition reaches 75-85% of pure-simulation throughput on a 36-core box. For a cheap bitboard game with Python orchestration, the data say the bottleneck is per-step Python/IPC overhead (Gymnasium/SB3 degrade above ~1000 synchronisations/s per core), so the highest-leverage move is a batched C++ (or Cython) env that steps all games in one call, with GPU-batched inference and double-buffering, before considering IMPALA-style asynchrony.

### Cited Findings

**EnvPool (C++ thread pool, batched async stepping)**
- "With 256 CPU cores on an NVIDIA DGX-A100 machine, EnvPool achieves a simulation throughput of one million frames per second on Atari and three million physics steps per second on MuJoCo environments, which is 14.9x / 19.2x improvement over the current popular Python implementation [4] (i.e., 72K frames per second / 163K physics steps per second)"; "On a laptop with 12 CPU cores, EnvPool obtains a speed 2.8x of the Python implementation." — [Weng et al. 2022, arXiv 2206.10558](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10558)
- Table 1 (FPS, Atari / MuJoCo): Laptop (12 cores) — For-loop 4,893 / 12,861; Subprocess 15,863 / 36,586; Sample-Factory 28,216 / 62,510; EnvPool sync 37,396 / 66,622; EnvPool async 49,439 / 105,126. Workstation (32 cores) — Subprocess 47,699 / 105,432; Sample-Factory 138,847 / 309,264; EnvPool sync 133,824 / 380,950; EnvPool async 200,428 / 582,446. DGX-A100 (256 cores) — Subprocess 71,943 / 163,656; Sample-Factory 707,494 / 1,573,262; EnvPool sync 427,851 / 949,787; EnvPool async 891,286 / 2,363,864; EnvPool numa+async 1,069,922 / 3,134,287. — [arXiv 2206.10558, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10558)
- "The most popular Subprocess implementation has extremely poor scalability with an almost flat curve"; "even if we use a single environment in EnvPool, we can get a free ~2x speedup"; "synchronous modes have significant performance disadvantages against asynchronous systems. This is because the throughput of the synchronous mode execution is determined by the slowest single environment instance". — [arXiv 2206.10558, Sec. 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10558)
- Design: "C++ threadpool-based executor engine"; async mode "receives a batched action through the send function and returns immediately"; batch_size M < num_envs N, "EnvPool only waits for the outputs of the first M environment steps"; "We recommend setting num_env N to be 2-3x greater than the number of threads". — [arXiv 2206.10558, Sec. 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10558)
- End-to-end effect with PPO (N=8 envs, CleanRL): "the end-to-end training time decreased from 200 minutes (CleanRL's PPO + For-loop) to approximately 73 minutes (CleanRL's PPO + EnvPool (Sync)) while maintaining sample efficiency." — [arXiv 2206.10558, Sec. 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10558)

**Sample Factory (APPO: async rollout workers, GPU policy workers, learner; double-buffered sampling)**
- Table 1, System #2 (36-core CPU, one RTX 2080 Ti), peak env frames/s (and % of pure simulation): Pure simulation 181,740 Atari / 322,907 VizDoom / 49,679 DMLab; DeepMind IMPALA 9,961 (5.3%) / 10,708 / 8,782; RLlib IMPALA 22,440 / 12,391 / 13,932; SeedRL V-trace 39,726 / 34,428 / 34,773; rlpyt PPO 68,880 / 73,544 / 32,948; SampleFactory APPO 135,893 (74.8%) / 146,551 (45.4%) / 42,149 (84.8%). — [Petrenko et al. 2020, arXiv 2006.11751, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11751)
- Architecture: "N parallel rollout workers simulate k environments each ... M policy workers, which generate actions and new hidden states via an accelerated forward pass on the GPU. Complete trajectories are sent from rollout workers to the learner"; "2 to 4 policy worker instances easily saturate the rollout workers with actions"; "Double-buffered sampling splits k environments on the rollout worker" so the GPU computes actions for one half while the CPU steps the other, "which practically eliminates idle time on CPU workers." — [arXiv 2006.11751, Sec. 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11751)
- Tuning facts: "The number of environments per core that enables the highest throughput lies between 24 and 25 for VizDoom"; "In all our experiments the policy lag was on average between 5 and 10 SGD steps, which results in stable training." — [arXiv 2006.11751, Appendix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11751)
- Python IPC is a real ceiling: "we found that at frame rates above 10^5 FPS even communicating these addresses can be difficult. In fact, at this speed the standard Python's multiprocessing.Queue tends to occupy a significant portion of CPU time" — they replaced it with a C++ FIFO on POSIX mutexes. — [arXiv 2006.11751, Appendix B.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11751)
- Off-policy handling: "we implemented both V-trace and PPO clipping in Sample Factory ... We find that a combination of PPO clipping and V-trace works well across tasks and yields stable training"; hyperparameters: rollout length 32, batch 2048 samples, 1 training epoch. — [arXiv 2006.11751, Sec. 3.4 and Table A.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11751)

**IMPALA (decoupled actors + GPU learner + V-trace)**
- Table 1 (DeepMind Lab, frames/s = 4x agent steps, shallow model): Single machine — A3C 32 workers (64 CPUs) 6.5K/9K; Batched A2C sync-step (48 CPUs, 0 GPU) 9K/5K; Batched A2C sync-step (1 GPU) 13K/5.5K; Batched A2C sync-traj 16K/17.5K; Batched A2C dyn-batch (1 GPU) 16K/13K; IMPALA 48 actors (0 GPU) 17K/20.5K; IMPALA dyn-batch 48 actors (1 GPU) 21K/24K. Distributed — A3C 200 CPUs 46K/50K; IMPALA 150 CPUs + 1 GPU 80K; IMPALA optimised 375 CPUs 200K; IMPALA optimised batch 128, 500 CPUs 250K. GPUs are P100. — [Espeholt et al. 2018, arXiv 1802.01561, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01561)
- "IMPALA achieves exceptionally high data throughput rates of 250,000 frames per second, making it over 30 times faster" than single-machine methods; "250,000 frames/sec or 21 billion frames/day". — [arXiv 1802.01561](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01561)
- Why sync batching loses: "the slowest environment in each batch determines the time it takes to perform the entire batch step ... high variance in environment speed can severely limit performance. Batched A2C works particularly well on Atari environments, because rendering and game logic are computationally very cheap in comparison to the number of frames per update". — [arXiv 1802.01561, Sec. 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01561)
- Off-policy correction matters once lag grows: Table 2 shows V-trace best "on 3 out of 5 tasks" without replay and "when the lag increases due to use of experience replay, V-trace performs better than all other methods in 4 out 5 tasks"; without lag "both V-trace and 1-step importance sampling perform similarly well". Unroll length 20 and batch 32 for DMLab; unroll 100 in Atari experiments. — [arXiv 1802.01561, Table 2 and appendix](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01561)

**PufferLib (shared-memory multiprocessing + its own EnvPool-style async pool; later native C envs)**
- Table 2 vectorised throughput, desktop (24-core i9-14900K) / laptop (6-core i7-10750H): Cartpole — PufferLib 460k / 110k, Puffer Pool 3M / 200k, Gymnasium 82k / 14k, SB3 100k / 11k; Ocean Squared — 490k / 110k, 4M / 190k, 116k / 12k, 99k / 12k; Atari Breakout — 11.8k / 2k, 25.6k / 3k, 4.8k / 2.5k, 3.3k / 2.3k; Nethack — 96k / 18k, Gymnasium 7k, SB3 6k. — [Suarez 2024, arXiv 2406.12905, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12905)
- "The Gymnasium and SB3 multiprocessing implementations experience significant scaling degradation above 1000 synchronizations per second per core. Instead of clogging the system with small processes, PufferLib provides an optimized implementation for running multiple environments/core."; "PufferLib's emulation layer typically adds a few tens of microseconds to simulation time"; shared memory for obs/rewards/terminals, "Zero-copy batching" by waiting on a contiguous subset of worker indices. — [arXiv 2406.12905, Sec. 3 and 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12905)
- Current PufferLib docs (GPU-native C/CUDA envs, a different regime): "Up to 60,000,000 step/second training in only ~10k lines of CUDA C"; "Within each buffer, environment execution is parallelized with OMP threading"; "Each buffer asynchronously queues data transfers to/from the GPU and uses pinned memory." — [puffer.ai docs](https://puffer.ai/docs.html)

**GPU/TPU-resident environments (Brax, Podracer/Anakin) — for contrast**
- Brax: "we can easily scale Brax up to hundreds of millions of steps per second of performance by distributing environment computation within and across accelerators" (TPUv3 8x8), versus "the typical use case of a practitioner running the OpenAI gym MuJoCo-ant on a single threaded machine (~thousands of steps per second)". — [Freeman et al. 2021, arXiv 2106.13281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13281)
- Podracer: Anakin keeps environments on TPU ("the environment computation is replicated across all available cores" via vmap/pmap); Sebulba "steps the environments on the host CPU, and splits the available 8 TPU cores (for each host) in two disjoint sets: A cores are used exclusively to act, and the remaining 8 - A cores are used to learn." — [Hessel et al. 2021, arXiv 2104.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06272)
- OpenAI Five's rollout design: games run "at approximately 1/2 real time, because we found that we could run slightly more than twice as many games in parallel at this speed"; "The rollout machines run the game engine but not the policy; they communicate with a separate pool of GPU machines which run forward passes in larger batches of approximately 60." — [arXiv 1912.06680, Sec. 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)

### Inferences
- The FoW pipeline's 800 decisions/s is 6x below EnvPool's *single-threaded For-loop* Atari rate on a laptop (4,893 FPS) and ~2 orders below what a 12-core laptop reaches with EnvPool async (49k). Since bitboard chess rules are cheaper than Atari emulation, the current ceiling is almost certainly Python per-step overhead and inference round-trips, not game logic.
- Best speedup per engineering hour, ordered by the evidence: (1) a batched C++/Cython env exposing `step(actions[N]) -> obs[N]` over all N games in one call (EnvPool's "free ~2x" even at N=1 and 15-19x at scale comes from removing Python/IPC per step; PufferLib shows Python multiprocessing collapses above ~1000 syncs/s/core); (2) double-buffered GPU inference so CPU stepping overlaps the policy forward pass (Sample Factory reaches 75-85% of pure-sim speed this way); (3) only then asynchronous actor/learner with V-trace + PPO clipping, which buys another 1.3-2x by removing the slowest-env stall but introduces policy lag (keep it to 5-10 SGD steps per Sample Factory).
- For 1 GPU and a few dozen CPU cores, synchronous "batched A2C/PPO sync-traj" with a C++ env is within ~1.3x of IMPALA on the same 48 CPUs (16K vs 17-21K in IMPALA Table 1), so synchronous PPO is a reasonable first stop; asynchrony pays most when env step time has high variance (variable-length FoW games with resets qualify, so use a persistent pool where finished games reset in place rather than a lockstep batch).
- Realistic target after a C++ batched env on a workstation-class CPU: 10^5 decisions/s (Sample Factory/EnvPool Atari-class numbers on 32-36 cores) is attainable for an 8x8 bitboard game, i.e. 100x over the current 800/s; on a small CPU count (e.g. 8-12 cores) expect 2-5x10^4/s. These are extrapolations from Atari/MuJoCo numbers, not chess measurements.

### Gaps
- No published throughput numbers for a batched chess/RBC environment (OpenSpiel RBC or python-chess based) were found; the Atari/MuJoCo numbers above are the only anchors.
- PufferLib's newer "Ocean" C environments and 60M steps/s claims are for GPU-native CUDA environments and do not transfer to a CPU chess simulator.

## Key question 3: Recurrent-policy training mechanics at scale (truncated BPTT length, burn-in, stored vs recomputed hidden state)

### Takeaway
R2D2's evidence (via secondary quotes) is that zero-initialised hidden states cause the most severe "recurrent state staleness", stored states fix most of it, burn-in alone only helps the early part of a sequence, and stored-state + burn-in (m = 80 with 40-step overlap/burn-in) is the combination R2D2 shipped. On-policy systems use short windows (OpenAI Five: 16-step unrolls; Sample Factory: 32-step rollouts with hidden states produced by the policy workers) and recurrent-PPO ablations favour longer sequences. A PPO implementation that reconstructs the full prefix under current parameters is the limit case of "infinite burn-in" and removes staleness entirely; the trade is compute, which can be bounded by recomputing only a fixed prefix.

### Cited Findings
- R2D2 design: "R2D2 stores fixed-length (m = 80) sequences of (s, a, r) in replay, with adjacent sequences overlapping each other by 40 time steps"; "built upon prioritized distributed replay and n-step double Q-learning (with n = 5), generating experience by a large number of actors (typically 256)"; target network copied "every 2500 learner steps"; "R2D2 utilizes two approaches: stored states and burn-in to mitigate" representational drift and recurrent state staleness. — [DI-engine R2D2 doc quoting Kapturowski et al. 2019](https://di-engine-docs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/12_policies/r2d2.html); original paper at [OpenReview r1lyTjAqYX](https://openreview.net/forum?id=r1lyTjAqYX) (inaccessible behind CAPTCHA at research time)
- Strategy definitions: stored state "Remedies the weakness of the zero start state strategy" but may "Suffer from the effect of 'representational drift' leading to 'recurrent state staleness'"; burn-in uses "a portion of the replay sequence only for unrolling the network and producing a start state, and update the network only on the remaining part of the sequence", so the network can "partially recover from a poor start state". — [UT Austin CS395T lecture deck on R2D2](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~pingali/CS395TFall25/presentations/cao_recurrentexperiencereplay.pdf)
- Q-value discrepancy findings (as summarised from the paper): "the zero start state heuristic results in a significantly more severe effect of recurrent state staleness"; "the burn-in strategy on its own partially mitigates the staleness problem on the initial part of replayed sequences, while not showing a significant effect on the Q-value discrepancy for later sequence states"; "Empirically, this translates into noticeable performance improvements"; "Stored-State strategy reduces the representation drift and improves the performance"; in the memory-length ablation "Stored State performance decreases more rapidly w.r.t. the reduction in history length ... the Stored State strategy is better at letting the agent use memory, compared to the zero state." — [UT Austin lecture deck](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~pingali/CS395TFall25/presentations/cao_recurrentexperiencereplay.pdf)
- Q-value discrepancy definition: "the difference between the Q value vector output using the hidden state and the stored recurrent states, normalized by the maximal Q-value", focusing on "the difference that results from applying the same network (parameterized by theta-hat) to the distinct recurrent states". — [UT Austin lecture deck](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~pingali/CS395TFall25/presentations/cao_recurrentexperiencereplay.pdf)
- OpenAI Five (PPO + LSTM): "We apply the Adam optimizer using truncated backpropagation through time over samples of 16 timesteps"; batch "grouped in unrolled LSTM windows of length 16"; rollouts "send data every 256 steps"; GAE lambda 0.95 so "the GAE rewards need to be smoothed over a number of timesteps ~ 1/lambda = 20; using 256 timesteps causes relatively little loss." Data staleness ablation (Figure 5b): "Training on stale rollout data causes" slower training; staleness measured in "queue length" of parameter versions. — [arXiv 1912.06680](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)
- Sample Factory: policy workers "generate actions and new hidden states via an accelerated forward pass on the GPU"; "The observations x_t and the hidden states of the agent h_t ... stored in shared tensors"; rollout length T = 32 with one epoch per batch (hidden states therefore come from a policy at most 5-10 SGD steps stale). — [arXiv 2006.11751](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11751)
- Recurrent PPO with truncated BPTT (Pleines et al. 2022): "The output hidden state of the previous sequence is the input hidden state of its consecutive one"; implementation pitfalls include "correctly sampling hidden states for sequence starts" and masking paddings; regarding burn-in they note it "remains unclear how an agent shall initialize its hidden state when acting without the experience replay buffer used during training"; grid search over sequence lengths {1, 8, 16, 32, 64, Max} found "longer sequences and greater hidden state sizes are favorable". — [Pleines et al. 2022, arXiv 2205.11104](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11104)
- DeepNash's learner processes full episodes in ordered chunks in reverse so values/state carry across chunks exactly: "splits them into the sequence of ordered chunks of fixed length (mini batches), and computes the parameter updates on that sequence in a reverse order. This allows computing the exact full returns and to carry over the necessary information between mini-batches". — [arXiv 2206.15378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)
- Burn-in tuning in supervised RNN training: "Proper tuning of the burn-in phase can lead to a reduction of prediction error on training and test data of more than 60% in some cases" (time-series, not RL). — [arXiv 2602.10911](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10911v1)

### Inferences
- What a PPO implementation with full-prefix reconstruction should adopt: full-prefix recomputation under the current parameters is strictly stronger than R2D2's stored-state + 40-step burn-in (it yields the state the *current* network would have had, so staleness is zero by construction); the cost is O(episode length) extra forward passes per training sequence. If that cost dominates, the evidence supports a fixed recomputed prefix (burn-in) of ~40 steps starting from the *stored* state rather than zero, which is exactly R2D2's final configuration; never start from zeros mid-episode.
- On-policy settings tolerate much shorter windows than R2D2's replay setting because the hidden states are at most a few SGD steps stale (Sample Factory 5-10 steps; OpenAI Five targets sample reuse ~1). With synchronous PPO and one epoch, stored states from the rollout are close to fresh and 16-32-step truncated windows have worked at scale; with several PPO epochs per batch, states go stale within the batch, which is where recomputation pays.
- FoW chess episodes (tens to a couple hundred plies) are short compared with DMLab/Dota horizons, so full-prefix reconstruction is cheap relative to those domains; DeepNash's "reverse-ordered chunk" learner is the closest published pattern for exact full-episode processing on a board game.

### Gaps
- The R2D2 primary text (exact Q-value discrepancy magnitudes, Atari/DMLab frame budgets, and the final-config table) could not be accessed; numbers above are second-hand quotes and should be re-verified against the OpenReview PDF before being used in a report.
- No published ablation compares stored-state vs full-prefix recomputation inside PPO; the Pleines grid only varies sequence length and hidden size with carried-over hidden states.

## Key question 4: CPU inference cost of a ~2M-parameter CNN+GRU under ONNX Runtime on 2 cores, and the realistic test-time search budget within 4 s/move

### Takeaway
No primary benchmark of a ~2M-parameter board CNN+GRU under ONNX Runtime on 2 cores was found; a FLOP-based estimate puts a single forward at roughly 3-10 ms and a batched forward at ~1-2 ms per position, implying a per-move budget on the order of 10^2-10^3 network evaluations in 4 s on 2 cores (inference, flagged below). Published anchors: AlphaZero evaluated 80k positions/s on 4 TPUs; penumbra (10-block ResNet, GPU batch 256) ran ~4,000 nodes/s; Lc0's CPU BLAS backend runs in the hundreds of nps on 6-16 desktop cores with its default (large) test net. ONNX Runtime defaults to one intra-op thread per physical core with spinning enabled, which matters when two sessions or search threads share 2 cores.

### Cited Findings
- ONNX Runtime threading defaults: intra_op_num_threads "Default: (not specified or 0)" yields a thread count matching physical cores with automatic affinitization; inter-op threads are created only in parallel execution mode and "by default, inter-op thread pool will also have one thread per physical core"; sequential execution is the default and ORT_PARALLEL helps only "when a model has many branches"; spinning "is enabled by default" and can be disabled with `session.intra_op.allow_spinning = 0`; for multiple sessions on few cores "customer might prefer their intra-op thread pools run on separate cores to avoid contention" via custom affinity. — [ONNX Runtime threading docs](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/performance/tune-performance/threading.html)
- AlphaZero evaluation throughput: "AlphaZero searches just 80 thousand positions per second in chess ... compared to 70 million for Stockfish"; "Each MCTS was executed on a single machine with 4 TPUs"; 800 simulations per move at 40 ms per move during training. — [arXiv 1712.01815](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815)
- Penumbra (RBC, 10-block ResNet with 8x8x104 input): "The program processes approximately 4,000 nodes per second" with batch size 256 and 4096 particles, on RTX 2080 Ti class hardware. — [Clark 2021, arXiv 2110.01810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01810)
- Lc0 CPU (BLAS backend, default pts/lczero test network, size not identified on the page): Zen 5 16c/32t "756 +/- 1 nps", 12c/24t "698 +/- 12 nps", 8c/16t "621 +/- 39 nps", 6c/12t "384 +/- 13 nps". — [OpenBenchmarking.org lczero test](https://openbenchmarking.org/test/pts/lczero)
- Lc0 CPU-only community guidance: smaller nets (e.g. "LD2 (10x128)", "11258-112x9-se", "11258-48x5-se") are preferred on CPU because "there's a sweet spot for CPU which depends on nps"; no nps figures given. — [TalkChess thread](https://talkchess.com/viewtopic.php?p=811101)
- RBC tournament clock the deployment must fit: "15 minutes per game plus 5 seconds per turn (sense and move), per player". — [PMLR 220](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/gardner23a.html)
- Sample Factory's observation that GPU batched inference, not CPU, is the practical path at training time: policy workers do "an accelerated forward pass on the GPU" and "2 to 4 policy worker instances easily saturate the rollout workers". — [arXiv 2006.11751](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11751)

### Inferences
- FLOP estimate (inference, not a cited measurement): a ~2M-parameter net where ~1.5-1.8M parameters are 3x3 convolutions applied over 64 squares costs ~2 x 1.7M x 64 ~ 220 MFLOP per forward, plus a GRU of a few hundred k parameters (~1 MFLOP) and heads. On 2 modern x86 cores with AVX2/AVX-512 FP32, ONNX Runtime's MLAS typically sustains tens of GFLOP/s per core on small convolutions, so a single-position forward lands around 3-10 ms, and batching 32-64 positions improves efficiency to roughly 1-2 ms per position. In a 4 s move budget that is ~400-1,300 sequential forwards, or ~2,000-4,000 positions if evaluations can be batched (e.g. evaluating many sampled boards from the belief state at once). The KataGo b6x96 net ("Strong/Top Amateur" in Go) is of a similar FLOP class, which supports keeping the deployed net in the 1-3M range.
- Consequence for search design: with 10^2-10^3 evaluations per move, AlphaZero-style MCTS with 800 sims per decision is at the edge of feasible only if the net is the sole evaluator and the belief is sampled sparsely; penumbra's design (particle belief of 4,096 boards, ~4,000 NN nodes/s on a GPU) shows that on CPU-only hardware the budget must be split between belief sampling and lookahead, favouring shallow, batched evaluation over deep trees.
- Thread policy: on exactly 2 cores, run one ORT session with intra_op_num_threads = 2 and either disable spinning or ensure no other busy thread competes, since ORT's default spinning threads will contend with a Python search loop; running the belief sampler in Python concurrently with inference will halve effective throughput.

### Gaps
- No published ms-per-forward figure for a ~2M-parameter 8x8 CNN+GRU under ONNX Runtime on 2 CPU cores was found; the estimate above must be replaced by a measured `onnxruntime_perf_test` number on the deployment CPU.
- Lc0's OpenBenchmarking numbers use an unidentified (large) default network and are not directly transferable to a 2M-parameter net; no CPU nps figures for Lc0's small nets (10x128 or smaller) were found in the threads accessed.
- No source was found on the GRU-specific cost in ORT (sequence length 1 at inference), though for a single step its cost is negligible relative to the conv trunk.
